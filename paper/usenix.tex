\subsection*{Abstract}

Regional capacity constraints and rising LLM inference demand are driving providers to pool GPU resources across
geographically distributed clusters.
Cross-region scheduling must navigate differences in WAN latency and regional
serving conditions.
Together, these regional differences and heterogeneous request-level SLOs make placement feasibility request-specific, so a region that is optional for one request may be the only SLO-feasible choice for another.
When available capacity is limited, immediately finalizing placement may leave no SLO-feasible capacity for later arrivals.
Waiting reveals more demand but leaves less time to meet the SLO.
Conventional cross-region schedulers make a one-shot placement for each request using only the demand
visible at arrival. Later arrivals cannot revise earlier placements, even when the affected requests have not yet
entered the serving engine.
The key observation is that every arrival can enter capacity planning immediately while placement remains revisable
until engine handoff.
We present RAVEL, an SLO-aware serving system that realizes this separation through tentative
placement, immediate demand accounting, and bounded pre-handoff replanning.
On a vLLM deployment with five serving replicas across three clusters connected by physical
WAN paths, RAVEL improves request-level SLO attainment over the best baseline
by 8.8, 11.2, and 13.4 percentage points on LMSYS, Burst, and DeepResearch,
respectively, while reducing P95 TTFT by 49.1--80.5\% and P95 end-to-end
latency by 15.5--56.8\%, at $16\times$ load.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Geo-distributed LLM serving expands available GPU capacity, but not every
region is equally useful to every request.
Large-scale private WANs expose heterogeneous inter-region paths
~\cite{b4,swan}, while distributed inference systems must additionally
contend with heterogeneous accelerator, serving, and network conditions
~\cite{helix,cassini,skylb,gorgo,solyx}.
Together with heterogeneous request-level SLOs, these differences make
regional capacity request-dependent: a region that is merely attractive for
one request may be the only SLO-feasible choice for another.
A placement therefore determines not only GPU load and cache locality, but
also the WAN path and remote serving conditions experienced by the request.
Interactive requests may impose tight time-to-first-token (TTFT) and
time-between-token (TBT) bounds, whereas longer-running or multi-stage
requests may instead be governed by end-to-end completion or
quality-of-experience objectives
~\cite{distserve,jitserve,quartz,andes,revisit_slo_goodput}.
Consequently, allocating regional capacity well requires reasoning about both
current serving conditions and the SLO-feasible alternatives available to
each request.

Existing distributed routers improve \emph{where} an arriving request should
execute using signals such as serving load, cache locality, WAN conditions,
request-level SLOs, and adaptive routing feedback
~\cite{preble,dualmap,skylb,gorgo,quartz,lodestar}.
However, these routing abstractions largely couple destination selection with
commitment: once a request is assigned using the currently visible state,
later arrivals generally do not influence that placement before execution
begins.
This coupling creates an additional online allocation problem.
A locally attractive placement for the current request can consume
SLO-feasible capacity that a later, more region-constrained request cannot
replace.
The missing question is therefore not only
\emph{where should this request execute?}, but also
\emph{when should an earlier placement become irreversible?}

A two-request example shows why early commitment can waste SLO-feasible
capacity.
Consider two serving regions, $A$ and $B$.
The first request, $r_1$, can satisfy its SLO in either region, whereas a
later request, $r_2$, can succeed only in $A$.
When $r_1$ arrives first, $A$ may appear faster and therefore be selected by a
locally optimal router.
If $r_2$ arrives shortly afterward, however, this decision has consumed its
only feasible capacity.
Placing $r_1$ in $B$ instead would allow both requests to succeed:
\emph{region $A$ is merely faster for $r_1$, but it is essential for $r_2$}.
Thus, optimizing requests independently can reduce aggregate request-level
SLO attainment even when each individual placement appears reasonable when
it is made.

Delaying commitment can reveal the second request, but observation consumes
the same SLO slack needed for WAN transfer, queueing, and inference.
A fixed-delay late-binding policy can therefore observe more demand before
placing a request, yet every millisecond of added deferral also reduces the
time remaining to satisfy that request's SLO.
We call this tension the \emph{information--slack tradeoff}.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} illustrates it empirically:
a short fixed deferral can expose useful arrivals and improve SLO attainment,
but the benefit saturates as the deferral consumes an increasing fraction of
request SLO budgets.
This leaves a design opportunity between immediate one-shot placement and
fixed-delay late binding: make arrived demand visible immediately while
preserving only bounded, request-specific placement revisability.

RAVEL's key insight is that deferred commitment does not require deferred
accounting.
Rather than treating placement as one atomic decision, RAVEL separates when a
request begins influencing capacity planning from when its destination becomes
final.
On arrival, RAVEL obtains replica-specific SLO quotes, selects a tentative
serving replica, and immediately attributes the request's prospective demand
to that replica.
Later arrivals therefore observe earlier demand even while the corresponding
placements remain revisable.
While an eligible request remains Router-owned, newly revealed demand may
trigger bounded replanning and transfer both its tentative ownership and
prospective charge to another replica.
The placement becomes final only after successful engine handoff.
By stopping reassignment at this explicit boundary, RAVEL preserves
pre-handoff flexibility without migrating engine-managed KV state.

Realizing this separation safely and efficiently requires three capabilities.
First, RAVEL must determine which replicas remain SLO-feasible under
heterogeneous WAN delays, engine backlog, Prefill and Decode interference, and
request-specific success criteria.
It addresses this challenge with calibrated, replica-specific SLO quotes
derived from live serving state.
Second, tentative work must affect subsequent decisions without becoming
double-counted or stale after reassignment.
RAVEL therefore maintains transferable prospective accounting whose ownership
and replica-dependent charges move atomically with each tentative placement.
Third, revisability must not require repeatedly optimizing over every
outstanding request or delaying work indefinitely.
RAVEL restricts expensive replanning to a bounded Router-owned cohort and ends
all placement revision at the engine-handoff boundary.
Together, these mechanisms retain useful online flexibility while bounding the
latency and state-management costs introduced by the control plane.

We implement RAVEL as an asynchronous routing layer for
vLLM~\cite{vllm} and evaluate it using five serving replicas across three
clusters connected by physical WAN paths.
Our experiments cover conversational traffic, bursty arrivals, and
completion-oriented demand over six offered-load levels.
At $16\times$ load, RAVEL improves request-level SLO attainment over DualMap
by \textbf{8.8}, \textbf{11.2}, and
\textbf{13.4 percentage points} on LMSYS, Burst, and DeepResearch,
respectively.
At the same load, it reduces P95 TTFT by
\textbf{49.1--80.5\%} relative to DualMap.
The online replanning path remains millisecond-scale, with a measured P95
scheduling-pass latency of 2.10\,ms at Burst $12\times$ load.

In summary, this paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
    We identify the \emph{request-dependent value of regional capacity} and
    the resulting \emph{information--slack tradeoff}: committing too early
    ignores demand revealed by later arrivals, whereas waiting too long
    consumes the SLO slack needed for successful execution.

    \item
    We introduce an \emph{immediate-accounting, deferred-commitment}
    abstraction and realize it through replica-specific SLO quotes,
    transferable prospective accounting, bounded pre-handoff replanning, and
    an explicit engine-handoff commitment boundary.

    \item
    We implement RAVEL on vLLM and evaluate it on a physical-WAN deployment,
    demonstrating consistent SLO-attainment improvements across
    conversational, bursty, and completion-oriented workloads while retaining
    millisecond-scale online planning overhead.
\end{itemize}




\section{Problem and Design Opportunity}
\label{sec:problem}

The design opportunity arises from separating three decisions that request
routing often couples: \emph{placement}, \emph{regional demand accounting},
and \emph{commitment}.
A placement associates a request with a candidate serving replica and,
therefore, with the region containing that replica.
Regional demand accounting attributes an arrived request's prospective work
to its tentative replica and region so that the request influences subsequent
placement decisions.
Commitment is the boundary after which the routing layer no longer revises
that placement.
Using only causally available information, the router seeks to maximize the
fraction of requests that satisfy all applicable request-level SLOs.
RAVEL chooses successful handoff to the serving engine as its commitment
boundary: a request remains reassignable while it is Router-owned and becomes
final only after handoff.
This is a deliberate control-plane boundary rather than an inherent
irreversibility property of LLM execution; it preserves placement flexibility
without requiring migration of engine-managed KV state.



\subsection{Request-Dependent Regional Feasibility}
\label{sec:problem:feasibility}

Prior SLO-aware serving systems show that whether a request can meet its
latency objective depends on both request-specific requirements and evolving
serving conditions~\cite{distserve,jitserve,quartz,andes}.
RAVEL makes this dependence explicit at the geographic placement layer:
SLO feasibility is specific to a request, candidate replica, and decision
time rather than a static property of a region.

Let $c$ denote a serving region, $\mathcal{R}_c$ the replicas in that region,
and $r\in\mathcal{R}_c$ a candidate replica.
For request $i$, let $\Phi_{i,r}(t)$ indicate whether replica $r$ is predicted,
using only information available at time $t$, to satisfy every SLO constraint
applicable to $i$.
This prediction depends on the request's SLO semantics and remaining slack,
its estimated service demand, the WAN path to region $c$, and the currently
observed serving state at replica $r$.
Because both serving state and remaining slack evolve over time,
$\Phi_{i,r}(t)$ is an online feasibility estimate rather than a guarantee:
future arrivals and prediction error may still change the realized outcome.
Section~\ref{sec:design:feasibility} describes how RAVEL constructs these
replica-level predictions.

We define the feasible-region set of request $i$ as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_i(t)
    =
    \left\{
        c
        \;\middle|\;
        \exists r\in\mathcal{R}_c:
        \Phi_{i,r}(t)=1
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:problem:feasible-set}
\end{equation}
The time index captures two distinct sources of change.
First, new arrivals and accounting updates alter the serving state visible to
the router, so $\mathcal{F}_i(t)$ may expand or shrink as contention changes.
Second, even if network and serving conditions remain unchanged, elapsed time
consumes TTFT or completion slack and can therefore remove regions from the
feasible set.

The size and composition of $\mathcal{F}_i(t)$ characterize how substitutable
regional capacity is for request $i$.
A request with several feasible regions has multiple serving alternatives,
whereas a request with a small feasible set depends on comparatively scarce
regional capacity.
For example, if
$\mathcal{F}_1=\{A,B\}$ and $\mathcal{F}_2=\{A\}$,
assigning request 1 to region $A$ may consume the only feasible option
available to request 2, even though request 1 could instead execute in $B$.
Thus, the opportunity cost of assigning capacity in a region depends on which
requests can substitute away from that region.
Regional capacity is therefore request-dependent, and optimizing
request--region assignments independently need not maximize aggregate
request-level SLO attainment.


\subsection{The Information--Slack Tradeoff}
\label{sec:problem:tradeoff}

The allocation problem is online: the router must make decisions without
observing future arrivals.
A placement that is reasonable under the currently visible state may become
undesirable after a more region-constrained request arrives.
This setting shares concerns with low-latency online and shared-state cluster
scheduling, where decisions must be made from incomplete and continually
changing information
~\cite{sparrow,flow_imprecise,omega,firmament}.
RAVEL does not assume an arrival oracle or attempt to reproduce an offline
optimal allocation; instead, it preserves bounded revisability using only
causally observed requests and serving state.

The relevant operating points differ along two independent axes:
when arrived demand becomes visible to capacity planning and when placement
becomes final.
Table~\ref{tab:problem:operating-points} separates these decisions.
A one-shot operating point assigns and accounts for a request immediately and
does not subsequently revise that placement.
At the other extreme, our diagnostic LateBind-$\tau$ policy keeps a request
centrally unassigned and unaccounted for a fixed interval $\tau$, then observes
the current state and makes a single final placement.
RAVEL occupies the remaining operating point: it makes the request's demand
visible immediately while retaining placement revisability until engine
handoff.

Deferring commitment can reveal information about later demand, but that
information is not free under request-level SLOs.
Delay scheduling similarly shows that waiting can expose better placement
opportunities~\cite{delayscheduling}, while deadline-driven scheduling
emphasizes the cost of consuming finite execution slack
~\cite{liu_layland_edf}.
Tail-sensitive distributed services further motivate treating latency slack
as a scarce resource rather than an unlimited observation window
~\cite{tail_at_scale}.
In geo-distributed LLM serving, these effects interact directly:
holding request $i$ for $\tau$ consumes $\tau$ of its available TTFT or
completion slack, leaving less time for WAN transfer, queueing, and inference.
A longer delay may therefore reveal more subsequent demand while
simultaneously reducing the set of regions in which the held request can still
succeed.
Equivalently, $\mathcal{F}_i(t)$ can shrink purely because the router waits,
even without an increase in serving load.

We refer to this tension as the \emph{information--slack tradeoff}.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} illustrates it empirically:
short fixed deferral can improve SLO attainment by exposing useful arrivals,
but the benefit saturates and eventually reverses as waiting consumes an
increasing fraction of request SLO budgets.
The design opportunity is therefore not simply to delay placement longer.
It is to make arrived demand visible immediately while deferring only
placement finality.

Exploiting this opportunity requires preserving useful revisability without
introducing unbounded waiting, inconsistent accounting, or unsafe ownership
transitions.
A practical design must
(i) estimate request-specific SLO feasibility,
(ii) account each tentative request exactly once and transfer its prospective
charge when placement changes,
(iii) bound both deliberate observation and multi-request replanning, and
(iv) enforce a failure-safe commitment boundary at engine handoff.
Section~\ref{sec:design} describes how RAVEL satisfies these requirements.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:design:overview}

RAVEL realizes immediate accounting and deferred commitment through two
ownership states separated by successful engine handoff.
While Router-owned, each request has exactly one tentative replica owner and
one set of prospective charges; after handoff, its placement is final and is
no longer eligible for Router-level reassignment.
This ownership boundary lets RAVEL exploit newly observed demand without
migrating engine-managed KV state.

Figure~\ref{fig:ravel-overview} summarizes the request lifecycle.
On arrival, RAVEL constructs request-specific SLO quotes for all candidate
replicas, selects an initial virtual placement, and immediately accounts the
request's prospective demand at that replica.
As later arrivals and engine feedback change the visible state, RAVEL may
replan a bounded cohort of still-Router-owned requests.
A reassignment transfers both tentative queue ownership and replica-dependent
prospective charges.
Requests cross a shallow, capacity-gated dispatch frontier before handoff.
A rejected handoff restores Router ownership, whereas successful handoff
transfers ownership to the serving engine and makes placement final.

The design maintains five invariants.
First, each Router-owned request has exactly one tentative owner.
Second, its prospective demand is represented exactly once and follows any
reassignment.
Third, only Router-owned requests may change placement.
Fourth, replanning uses only causally observed requests and serving state and
is bounded in both observation time and cohort size.
Finally, successful engine handoff is the only commitment event.
The following subsections describe replica quotes, prospective accounting,
bounded replanning, unified placement and reversible dispatch, and the
handoff boundary that enforces these invariants.


```latex
\subsection{Replica SLO Quotes}
\label{sec:design:feasibility}

Every placement decision consumes a \emph{replica SLO quote}: a causal,
request-specific summary of whether a candidate replica is predicted to
satisfy the request's SLO and of the cost of serving it there.
Quotes provide a common placement interface across heterogeneous request
semantics without collapsing TTFT, TBT, and completion constraints into an
arbitrary weighted score.
Prior phase-aware serving systems show that Prefill and Decode have distinct
resource demands and can interfere under shared execution
~\cite{sarathi,distserve,splitwise,shuffleinfer}.
RAVEL incorporates these effects into replica-specific feasibility estimates
rather than introducing a new phase scheduler.

Let $c\in\mathcal{C}$ denote a serving cluster,
$\mathcal{R}_c$ its replicas, $c(r)$ the cluster containing replica $r$, and
$\mathcal{R}=\bigcup_c\mathcal{R}_c$.
For request $i$ and candidate replica $r$, RAVEL constructs
\begin{equation}
    Q_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \left(
        \Phi_{i,r}(t),
        \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}(t),
        \mathbf{z}_{i,r}(t)
    \right),
    \label{eq:ravel-quote}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{i,r}(t)$ indicates whether all SLO constraints applicable to
request $i$ are predicted to hold,
$\widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}(t)$ is the request-class-specific predicted
objective used for ordering and slack calculations, and
$\mathbf{z}_{i,r}(t)$ contains secondary capacity-pressure signals used by the
placement policy.
The quote depends only on information causally available at time $t$ and is an
online prediction rather than a formal SLO guarantee.

RAVEL combines calibrated cluster-level service profiles with replica-local
live and prospective state.
At active Decode concurrency $n$, it models Prefill service for a prompt of
size $p$ as
$P_{c,n}(p)=b_{c,n}+a_{c,n}p$ and uses $d_c(n)$ for the calibrated
post-first-token Decode time per token.
For request $i$ and candidate replica $r$, let
$\widehat{S}^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,r}$ denote its predicted Prefill-side service
delay.
This estimate includes live engine Prefill debt, Router-owned prompt work ahead
of $i$, the request's own prompt demand, and tentative assignments selected
earlier in the current planning pass.
RAVEL then predicts
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    (t-a_i)
    +
    \rho_{o_i,c(r)}
    +
    \widehat{S}^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,r},
    \label{eq:ravel-ttft}
\end{equation}
where $a_i$ is the request arrival time and
$\rho_{o_i,c(r)}$ is the directed WAN delay from request origin $o_i$ to
cluster $c(r)$.
The elapsed term $(t-a_i)$ directly charges time already spent under Router
ownership against the remaining TTFT budget.
Thus, even with unchanged WAN and serving conditions, a previously feasible
replica may become infeasible as observation consumes request slack.

For a LATENCY request with TTFT and TBT bounds
$D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}}$ and $D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}$, RAVEL evaluates the two
constraints independently:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{\mathrm{lat}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}(t)
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}}
        \;\land\;
        d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r})
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}
    \right],
    \label{eq:ravel-latency-feasible}
\end{equation}
where $m_{i,r}$ conservatively summarizes live and prospective Decode
concurrency while logically deduplicating request identifiers.
Feasibility therefore requires both bounds to hold: a lower predicted TTFT
cannot compensate for a predicted TBT violation.

Completion-oriented requests instead require a prediction of total
request-visible service time.
RAVEL distinguishes expected output demand
$L_i^{\mathrm{exp}}$ from a conservative risk estimate
$L_i^{\mathrm{risk}}$.
Let $\widehat{T}^{\mathrm{risk}}_{i,r}(t)$ denote the predicted completion
latency obtained from TTFT and the Decode service implied by
$L_i^{\mathrm{risk}}$.
Completion feasibility is
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{\mathrm{comp}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{risk}}_{i,r}(t)
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{comp}}
    \right].
    \label{eq:ravel-completion-feasible}
\end{equation}
The risk estimate gates feasibility, while the expected estimate contributes
to shared service-pressure accounting.
Separating the two avoids using an optimistic estimate to admit unsafe
placements while also avoiding systematic over-accounting of prospective
demand.
Under sustained completion pressure, RAVEL may instead use a held-out,
request-visible expected-output profile when constructing completion quotes;
Section~\ref{sec:design:policy} describes this adaptation.
Neither future arrivals nor the evaluated request's realized output length is
visible to the Router.

Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:quotes} gives the complete quote equations,
service-profile calibration procedure, runtime source of every quote term,
output-demand fallbacks, and the exact construction of
$\widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}$ and $\mathbf{z}_{i,r}$.
```

\subsection{Immediate and Transferable Demand Accounting}
\label{sec:design:accounting}

Revisability is safe only if tentative demand remains visible exactly once.
RAVEL therefore associates every Router-owned request with one tentative
replica queue and one set of replica-dependent prospective charges.
This makes the request visible immediately without making its placement
final.

When request $i$ receives a virtual placement at replica $r$, it enters
$r$'s Router-owned queue.
Its prompt contributes to the queue-ahead Prefill work observed by later
quotes, including quotes constructed later in the same planning pass.
The planner additionally maintains lightweight signals for profiled
completion-service pressure and LATENCY/TBT pressure.
These prospective signals complement rather than replace live engine pending
and running state.

If request $i$ moves from replica $r$ to $r'$, RAVEL transfers its queue
ownership and recomputes all replica-dependent charges for the new target.
For a virtual ledger $Z$ with request-specific charge $z_{i,r}$, the transfer
is conceptually
\begin{equation}
    Z_r \leftarrow Z_r-z_{i,r},
    \qquad
    Z_{r'} \leftarrow Z_{r'}+z_{i,r'}.
    \label{eq:ravel-account-transfer}
\end{equation}
The source removal and destination addition occur atomically on the
serialized control path.
A planning snapshot therefore never observes the request at both replicas or
at neither replica.
The request joins the destination's normal planning order rather than
retaining its previous physical queue position.

Successful handoff ends Router queue ownership but does not erase the
request's effect on later decisions.
Its active-service charge remains until terminal completion, while
replica-reported pending and running state continues to inform subsequent
quotes.
Request identifiers are logically deduplicated wherever live and prospective
state refer to the same request.
Rejected handoff restores Router ownership and its prospective state; terminal
completion releases the tracked charge and may trigger another planning pass.

Together, queue ownership and prospective accounting establish RAVEL's central
accounting invariant:
\emph{tentative work is immediately visible, but visibility does not make its
placement final}.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:accounting} specifies the exact charges, lifecycle
operations, atomicity rules, and stale-callback protection.


\subsection{Bounded Observation and Replanning}
\label{sec:design:replanning}

RAVEL replans only when newly observed information may change an uncommitted
assignment, and it bounds both the movable set and the observation delay.
A trigger starts a planning pass, eligibility determines which requests may
move, and neither event changes the commitment boundary.

Only Router-owned requests are eligible for reassignment.
Committed engine-owned work remains fixed but continues to affect later
quotes.
RAVEL also excludes requests with no alternative SLO-feasible target.
Planning is event-driven rather than continuous: arrivals, mobility-hold
expiration, relevant engine feedback, request termination, and rejected
handoff may request a pass.
Multiple requests for replanning are serialized and may be coalesced.

\paragraph{Slack-bounded mobility hold.}
For selected non-LATENCY requests, RAVEL preserves a short observation window
before dispatch.
The request is already virtually placed and accounted; the hold only keeps it
Router-owned long enough for nearby arrivals to expose whether its tentative
capacity assignment should change.

Let
$\sigma_i^{\mathrm{arr}}=
\max\{0,\Delta_i-\min_r\widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}(a_i)\}$
denote arrival-time planning slack, where $\Delta_i$ is the applicable TTFT or
completion planning budget.
For a hold-eligible request,
\begin{equation}
    h_i
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \min\!\left\{
            \rho_i^{\min},
            \sigma_i^{\mathrm{arr}}
        \right\},
        & \text{if $i$ is hold-eligible},\\
        0,
        & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:ravel-mobility-hold}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_i^{\min}$ is the minimum positive RTT to a remote serving cluster.
Eligibility requires a causally detected RTT-scale burst, at least two
SLO-feasible regions, and a non-LATENCY request.
LATENCY requests always use $h_i=0$.
The RTT bound limits deliberate observation to a network timescale, while the
slack bound prevents the hold from intentionally exceeding the request's
predicted planning slack.

Hold expiration is not commitment.
It makes a request dispatch-eligible, but the request remains revisable until
successful handoff.
This distinguishes RAVEL from fixed-delay late binding: held requests are
already placed and accounted throughout the observation interval.

\paragraph{Bounded cohort.}
Each planning pass orders movable requests by
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_i
    =
    (a_i+\Delta_i,\;a_i,\;id_i)
    \label{eq:ravel-planning-order}
\end{equation}
and ordinarily considers a prefix of at most $K$ candidates.
Requests outside the cohort retain their tentative owners and enter the
planning snapshot as fixed context.
Within the cohort, every selected placement immediately updates pass-local
prospective state before the next candidate is evaluated.

Under completion pressure, the effective bound expands from $K$ to the
deployment's finite aggregate sequence capacity.
With $R=|\mathcal{R}|$ candidate replicas and effective cohort bound
$K_{\mathrm{eff}}$, one greedy pass performs
$O(K_{\mathrm{eff}}R)$ request--replica quote comparisons.
Completion pressure changes the amount of Router-owned state reconsidered,
not the placement algorithm or ownership boundary.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ravel-control} summarizes the transitions that must
preserve RAVEL's invariants: immediate arrival-time accounting, bounded
reassignment, and failure-safe handoff.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{\textsc{RAVEL} Router Control Loop}
\label{alg:ravel-control}
\small

\Fn{\textnormal{\textsc{OnArrival}}($i$)}{
    $Q_i \gets \textsc{QuoteReplicas}(i)$\;
    $r_i \gets \textsc{InitialVirtualPlacement}(i,Q_i)$\;
    \textsc{AssignAndAccount}($i,r_i$)\;
    \tcp{Demand becomes visible immediately}

    $h_i \gets \textsc{MobilityHold}(i,Q_i)$\;
    \If{$h_i=0$ \textbf{or} \textsc{CompletionPressure}()}{
        \textsc{RequestReplan}()\;
    }
    \Else{
        \textsc{ArmReplan}($h_i$)\;
    }
}

\BlankLine

\Fn{\textnormal{\textsc{Replan}}()}{
    $C \gets \textsc{BoundedMovableCohort}()$\;
    \tcp{Only Router-owned work is revisable}
    $X \gets \textsc{SLOAwareGreedyPlace}(C)$\;
    \For{$i\in C$}{
        \textsc{TransferOrRefresh}($i,X_i$)\;
    }
}

\BlankLine

\Fn{\textnormal{\textsc{Dispatch}}($i$)}{
    \If{\textsc{Held}($i$) \textbf{or} \textsc{FrontierBlocks}($i$)}{
        \Return\;
    }

    $r \gets \textsc{CurrentTentativeOwner}(i)$\;
    \textsc{RemoveFromRouterQueue}($i$)\;

    \ElseIf{$s=\textsc{Committed}$}{
        \textsc{FinalizePlacement}($i,r$)\;

    }
    \Else{
        \textsc{SurfaceFailureAfterCleanup}($i$)\;
    }
}

\subsection{Unified SLO-Aware Placement and Reversible Dispatch}
\label{sec:design:policy}

Replica quotes identify which targets remain SLO-feasible; the placement
policy determines how scarce feasible capacity is allocated among them.
RAVEL uses one deterministic lexicographic policy for both initial placement
and bounded cohort replanning.
Request classes affect the feasibility and objective fields exposed by a
quote, but do not change the placement algorithm that consumes it.

\paragraph{Initial and cohort placement.}
On arrival, RAVEL constructs a quote for every candidate replica.
If at least one replica is predicted feasible, RAVEL selects among feasible
targets using capacity-pressure, efficiency, and stability signals.
If no replica is predicted feasible, it first minimizes normalized predicted
SLO violation and only then considers lower-priority efficiency signals.
Unlike cache-aware routers that jointly optimize reusable KV state and load
~\cite{preble,dualmap}, RAVEL treats prefix locality as a late tie-breaker:
cache affinity cannot override request-level SLO feasibility.

Replanning applies the same placement policy to a bounded movable cohort.
Requests are processed in the order defined by
Equation~\ref{eq:ravel-planning-order}.
Each request is evaluated against the fixed system context together with the
tentative assignments already selected earlier in the current pass.
After selecting a target, RAVEL immediately updates the pass-local
prospective state before evaluating the next request.
Later requests therefore observe the demand induced by earlier tentative
choices rather than reasoning from a stale pre-pass snapshot.

The placement key has three lexicographic tiers.
First, \emph{SLO safety} prioritizes predicted feasibility and, when no target
is feasible, minimizes normalized predicted violation.
Second, \emph{capacity pressure} considers prospective sequence-capacity
headroom, profiled completion-service pressure, and LATENCY/TBT pressure so
that broadly substitutable work does not unnecessarily consume scarce
SLO-feasible capacity.
Third, \emph{efficiency and stability} consider the request-specific predicted
objective, marginal service cost, placement movement, prefix locality, and
deterministic replica identity.
Because these tiers are lexicographically ordered, a lower-priority load,
locality, or stability benefit cannot compensate for an avoidable SLO miss.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:placement-key} gives the exact implementation key,
normalization rules, and tie-breaking direction.

\paragraph{Completion-pressure adaptation.}
Completion pressure changes the amount of Router-owned work reconsidered and
the completion-demand estimate supplied to the common planner; it does not
introduce a second placement or dispatch policy.
Using only causally observed arrivals and serving state, RAVEL detects
sustained workflow-completion pressure and expands the movable cohort from
the ordinary bound $K$ to the deployment's finite aggregate sequence
capacity.
When a held-out semantic profile indexed only by request-visible workflow
metadata is available, RAVEL may also replace the ordinary conservative
output-demand estimate with the profile's expected-output estimate when
constructing completion quotes.
These adaptations change planner inputs and the amount of reconsidered state,
not the placement algorithm itself.
Neither exposes future arrivals or the evaluated request's realized
generation length, and all requests continue through the same planner and
reversible dispatch path.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:completion-pressure} gives the exact pressure
predicate, entry and exit conditions, cohort expansion, and profile
fallbacks.

\paragraph{Shallow reversible dispatch frontier.}
Placement revisability is useful only while work remains under Router
ownership.
RAVEL therefore limits how much work may cross from Router-owned state into
engine-facing state at once.
The dispatch frontier exposes only enough Prefill work to cover one calibrated
engine quantum and a bounded control horizon, subject to current engine
headroom.
Excess work remains in Router-owned queues, where it continues to contribute
to prospective accounting and remains eligible for replanning.

The frontier is a capacity-gated dispatch boundary rather than a second
admission or predicted-miss withholding policy.
A dispatch-eligible request at the frontier proceeds whenever its tentative
target has sufficient engine headroom.
Thus, the frontier bounds the amount of work approaching commitment while
allowing eligible work to progress whenever capacity is available.
It preserves Router-level visibility and revisability without creating a
protected cohort or a separate placement policy.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:frontier} specifies the exact frontier construction,
headroom test, wake-up events, and concurrent-handoff bound.

\paragraph{Engine-local TBT protection.}
Replica-level feasibility is meaningful only if local execution preserves the
service assumptions used to construct the corresponding quote.
Prior phase-aware serving systems mitigate Prefill--Decode interference
through chunking, disaggregation, or related execution controls
~\cite{sarathi,distserve,splitwise,shuffleinfer}.
RAVEL uses a narrower engine-local mechanism to preserve its predicted TBT
conditions rather than introducing another geographic scheduling decision.

When Chunked Prefill is enabled and LATENCY Decode is active, RAVEL exports
the tightest applicable TBT requirement together with deadline metadata to the
local serving adapter.
The adapter combines this requirement with a conservative calibrated Prefill
envelope to bound the next Prefill quantum.
LATENCY requests receive deadline/TBT-aware local priority, whereas
non-LATENCY work retains stable FCFS behavior.

These controls enforce the execution assumptions underlying replica quotes;
they do not alter geographic placement or create a second Router-level
scheduler.
The Router remains solely responsible for regional placement and pre-handoff
reassignment.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:tbt-control} gives the conditional activation rule,
chunk-bound equation, priority encoding, and scheduler wrapper.

\subsection{Commitment and Engine Handoff}
\label{sec:design:commitment}

Commitment is an ownership transition rather than a timer or queue event.
Only successful engine handoff transfers a request from Router-owned,
revisable state to engine-owned, final state.
Tentative placement, mobility-hold expiration, dispatch eligibility, and
temporary removal from a Router queue are all non-commitment events.

For a dispatch-eligible request, RAVEL reads its current tentative owner,
removes it from that owner's Router queue, and asks the target replica to
register the request.
Registration alone does not commit placement.
If the target rejects registration, RAVEL restores Router ownership and
prospective state at the tentative replica, after which the request again
follows the ordinary dispatch and replanning rules.

After registration succeeds, RAVEL creates and tracks the corresponding
engine-facing posting task.
Successful registration together with successful posting-task creation
constitutes \emph{successful handoff}.
Only then does the virtual placement become final.
If posting-task creation fails before this boundary, RAVEL aborts the partial
registration, cleans attempt-specific state, and surfaces the failure without
reporting a committed placement.

Failures after successful handoff belong to the committed attempt.
RAVEL performs terminal abort and cleanup and refreshes its planning state, but
does not reinterpret a post-handoff failure as another placement opportunity.
Attempt identifiers prevent delayed callbacks from an earlier attempt from
modifying ownership or accounting associated with a newer attempt.

Committed work remains visible to later planning.
Replica pending and running state contributes to later quotes, and its tracked
active-service charge persists until terminal completion.
Releasing that charge may request another planning pass so that newly
available capacity can affect requests that remain Router-owned.

RAVEL therefore separates three lifecycle events:
\emph{arrival makes demand visible; Router ownership keeps placement
revisable; successful engine handoff makes placement final}.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:handoff} gives the complete ownership state machine,
failure transitions, cleanup rules, and attempt-identifier semantics.



\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

We implement RAVEL in Python as an asynchronous control layer in front of
geo-distributed vLLM serving replicas~\cite{vllm}.
Our implementation targets vLLM~0.8.5.post1 and its frozen V0 scheduler
contract.
All control-plane operations that modify Router-owned queues, perform
replanning, or initiate dispatch are serialized by a single
\texttt{asyncio} scheduler lock.
New arrivals, mobility-hold expirations, engine feedback, and explicit
replanning requests therefore enter the same serialized scheduling path,
preventing concurrent passes from racing on tentative ownership or
prospective accounting.
Terminal callbacks may asynchronously release tracked service charges, but
any resulting replanning again enters this serialized path.

\paragraph{Serving profiles.}
RAVEL derives serving estimates from externally calibrated schema-v2 profiles
rather than hard-coded token rates.
Each profile carries an engine fingerprint and is bound to one serving cluster;
formal runs require a valid external profile for every cluster.
At runtime, RAVEL linearly interpolates Prefill and Decode measurements between
calibrated concurrency points and clamps queries outside the measured range to
the nearest endpoint.
The engine adapter additionally constructs a conservative Prefill envelope
from the maximum calibrated slope and intercept across concurrency levels.
For multi-stage completion requests, the Router may also load an offline
semantic output profile indexed only by request-visible workflow metadata,
such as $(\textit{stage\_id},\textit{stage\_num})$.
Neither profile exposes future arrivals or realized output length.

\paragraph{vLLM integration and local TBT control.}
RAVEL integrates with vLLM without modifying its source tree.
At process startup, a lightweight adapter feature-detects and wraps
\texttt{Scheduler.\_get\_priority} and
\texttt{Scheduler.\_schedule\_chunked\_prefill}.
If either required hook is unavailable, startup fails rather than silently
disabling RAVEL's local controls.
For LATENCY requests, the adapter encodes a Router-derived deadline-order key
together with the applicable TBT requirement in a reserved priority range;
non-LATENCY requests in the mixed path retain ordinary FCFS priority.
When LATENCY Decode is active, the adapter combines the tightest active TBT
requirement with the conservative Prefill envelope to derive a block-aligned
bound on the next Prefill quantum.
It temporarily reduces \texttt{max\_num\_batched\_tokens} for that
Chunked-Prefill scheduling iteration and restores the configured value in a
\texttt{finally} block.
The bound therefore affects only the current local scheduling decision and
does not introduce a separate Router-level placement policy.

\paragraph{Asynchronous handoff and failure handling.}
Engine handoff is implemented as an explicit asynchronous ownership
transition.
After Router-side admission and budget checks succeed, the target replica
first registers the request, after which RAVEL creates and tracks the
corresponding engine-facing posting task.
Successful replica registration together with successful posting-task
creation constitutes the handoff defined in
Section~\ref{sec:design:commitment}; only then does placement become final.

If replica registration is rejected, the request is restored to its
Router-owned queue together with its prospective state and remains eligible
for the ordinary mobility and replanning rules.
Failures while creating or executing an engine-facing posting task are
surfaced to the control plane rather than silently converted into new
Router-level placements.
Posting attempts carry attempt identifiers so that delayed callbacks from an
older attempt cannot modify ownership or accounting associated with a newer
one.

After successful handoff, replica-reported pending and running state continues
to inform subsequent Router quotes.
The corresponding active-service charge remains visible until terminal
completion.
At request termination, RAVEL releases that charge and requests another
serialized scheduling pass so that newly available capacity can affect
requests that remain Router-owned.

  \paragraph{Workloads and SLOs.}
  We use three workloads that expose different sources of placement uncertainty.
  \emph{LMSYS} represents heterogeneous conversational traffic.
  \emph{Burst} preserves the request contents of its source trace but replaces

  \emph{DeepResearch} represents multi-stage completion workflows whose output

  Each request carries an SLO class and a request-specific multiplier.
  The base budgets are 0.8\,s for TTFT, 80\,ms for TBT, and 8\,s for completion
  latency, after which the configured multiplier is applied.
  A LATENCY request succeeds only if it satisfies both its TTFT bound and every
  TBT bound.
  THROUGHPUT and COLLECTIVE requests succeed if their end-to-end completion
  latency remains within their completion budget.

  \paragraph{Replay protocol.}
  We replay each workload in open loop.
  At load factor $x$, every original inter-arrival interval is divided by $x$
  while request order, contents, output metadata, and SLOs are preserved.
  Before each cell, we reset replica prefix caches and start a fresh policy run.
  All policies receive the same request-visible information and are evaluated
  under the same model, topology, WAN paths, and replay schedule.
  The main matrix contains one 500-request replay per cell.
  The repeated-run campaign is conducted separately using five paired replays at
  $16\times$ load.

  We compare RAVEL with RoundRobin (RR), LeastLoad (LL), RD, DualMap, and
  SkyWalker.
  For the single-run headline results, the reported numerical gains use DualMap
  as the comparison policy.
  The repeated-run figure follows the designated best-baseline protocol used by
  that campaign.

  Our primary metric is request-level SLO attainment.
  Secondary metrics are time to first token (TTFT), end-to-end latency (E2E), and
  time per output token (TPOT).
  TTFT and E2E are measured from arrival at the Router, including time spent
  under Router ownership as well as WAN and engine delay.

  \subsection{End-to-End Performance}
  \label{sec:eval:e2e}

  RAVEL's advantage grows with contention, when a placement selected from
  arrival-time state is more likely to become stale before engine handoff.
  As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:main-results}, RAVEL maintains the highest
  request-level SLO attainment across the evaluated load sweep.

  At $16\times$ load, RAVEL improves SLO attainment over DualMap by
  8.8 percentage points on LMSYS, 11.2 percentage points on Burst, and
  13.4 percentage points on DeepResearch.
  The corresponding P95 TTFT reductions range from 49.1\% to 80.5\%, while
  P95 E2E reductions range from 15.5\% to 56.8\%.

  These gains are consistent with RAVEL retaining the ability to revise a
  tentative placement while the request remains Router-owned.
  As load increases, queueing and service estimates change more rapidly, making
  an irrevocable decision based only on arrival-time state increasingly costly.
  RAVEL can respond to these changes until handoff while prospective demand
  accounting remains active from the moment of arrival.

  The $1\times$ distributions in Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} show why raw
  latency alone is not sufficient to characterize this objective.
  The policies' latency distributions overlap for several metrics, yet RAVEL
  can still attain more request-level SLOs.
  It selectively uses available slack: a request with sufficient slack may
  tolerate a small additional delay if doing so preserves a scarce
  SLO-feasible replica for a more region-constrained request.
  Thus, the relevant objective is not to minimize every individual latency
  sample, but to allocate heterogeneous regional capacity so that more requests
  satisfy their own SLOs.

  \subsection{Mechanism Contributions}
  \label{sec:eval:mechanism}

  Both pre-handoff revisability and engine-local TBT protection contribute to
  RAVEL's end-to-end SLO gains.
  We isolate these mechanisms on Burst at $12\times$, where burst-driven queue
  changes are pronounced but the system has not collapsed into universal
  overload.

  We compare full RAVEL with two variants.
  \emph{FixedPlacement} retains RAVEL's SLO-aware initial placement,
  prospective accounting, and engine-local TBT guard, but disables reassignment
  after the initial choice.
  It therefore isolates the value of keeping placement revisable.
  \emph{NoTBTGuard} retains RAVEL's global routing, prospective accounting, and
  replanning, but disables the engine-local Prefill control used to protect
  active LATENCY Decode requests.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_mechanism_breakdown.pdf}

  Full RAVEL attains 81.2\% request-level SLO attainment.
  FixedPlacement attains 68.0\%, a reduction of 13.2 percentage points, while
  NoTBTGuard attains 71.4\%, a reduction of 9.8 percentage points.
  Thus, neither a strong initial placement nor global replanning without local
  TBT protection is sufficient.

  The component-level metrics explain these losses.
  FixedPlacement obtains a lower P95 TTFT than RAVEL
  (2.490 versus 2.905\,s), but its LATENCY-request TBT-violation rate increases
  from 6.6\% to 37.6\%.
  Its P95 E2E also rises to 21.347\,s, compared with 18.985\,s for RAVEL.
  An irrevocable geographic choice can therefore create downstream Decode
  pressure that the remaining local mechanism cannot fully repair.

  NoTBTGuard reduces P95 TTFT further to 1.951\,s and has nearly the same P95
  E2E as RAVEL (18.958 versus 18.985\,s), yet its TBT-violation rate reaches
  42.0\%.
  Optimizing aggregate TTFT or E2E is therefore insufficient when individual
  requests also carry inter-token deadlines.

  Because FixedPlacement retains prospective accounting, this experiment
  specifically measures the contribution of placement revisability while holding
  that accounting mechanism fixed.
  It should not be interpreted as an independent ablation of prospective
  accounting itself.

  \subsection{Quote Accuracy}
  \label{sec:eval:quote}

  RAVEL's quotes are useful only if they preserve the feasibility decision for
  each request, rather than merely achieving a small average prediction error.
  In the workload-$16\times$ analysis, the empirical false-feasible and
  false-infeasible rates are 6.2\% and 1.0\%, respectively.

  For a selected final-handoff record $i$, we define normalized quote error as
  \[
    e_i =
    \frac{\widehat{L}_i-L_i}{B_i},
  \]
  where $\widehat{L}_i$ is the predicted latency, $L_i$ is the observed latency,
  and $B_i$ is the corresponding request-specific SLO budget.
  For LATENCY requests, $L_i$ denotes TTFT; for completion-oriented requests,
  $L_i$ denotes E2E.
  Negative error is optimistic, whereas positive error is conservative.

  \begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{quote_accuracy_figure.pdf}
    \caption{
    \textbf{Quote accuracy in the workload-$16\times$ analysis.}
    Panel (a) shows the empirical CDF of normalized quote error
    $(\widehat{L}_i-L_i)/B_i$ for LATENCY (TTFT) and completion (E2E) requests.
    The shaded band denotes errors within $\pm10\%$ of the applicable SLO
    budget.
    Panel (b) reports feasibility-classification error rates over the same
    $N=2{,}500$ selected final-handoff records.
    Among 1,672 quoted-feasible decisions, 104 are false feasible (6.2\%);
    among 828 quoted-infeasible decisions, 8 are false infeasible (1.0\%).
    }
    \label{fig:quote-accuracy}
  \end{figure}

  Figure~\ref{fig:quote-accuracy} shows that most normalized errors remain near
  zero, although completion quotes exhibit a longer optimistic tail.
  More importantly, the quotes usually preserve the decision-relevant
  feasibility classification.
  Of the 1,672 records quoted as feasible, 104 later violate the corresponding
  SLO, giving a 6.2\% empirical false-feasible rate.
  Of the 828 records quoted as infeasible, only 8 complete feasibly, giving a
  1.0\% empirical false-infeasible rate.

  These measurements evaluate the placements RAVEL actually selected and handed
  off; they do not observe counterfactual execution on unselected replicas.
  The reported rates therefore characterize the empirical quality of the online
  decision signal, rather than providing a formal guarantee that every feasible
  quote will meet its SLO.

  \subsection{Planner Overhead}
  \label{sec:eval:overhead}

  RAVEL's replanning cost is small on the actual online path, while a controlled
  benchmark shows how planner latency scales with the bounded cohort size.
  We denote by $K$ the candidate cohort bound for one replanning pass; the
  normal online configuration caps it at $K=64$.

  \begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig4_planner_overhead.pdf}
    \caption{
    \textbf{Planner overhead on the five-replica testbed.}
    Panel (a) reports median planner latency as the candidate cohort bound
    $K$ increases on a $\log_2$ scale.
    The shaded bands span P5--P95 over 50 measured repetitions per point after
    five warm-up repetitions; the dashed line marks the configured online
    bound $K=64$, while $K=128$ is a stress-only point.
    Panel (b) shows the empirical CDF of scheduling-pass latency for 281 actual
    passes from Burst at $12\times$ load.
    }
    \label{fig:planner-overhead}
  \end{figure}

  On the actual Burst $12\times$ execution path, a scheduling pass takes
  0.998\,ms at the median and 2.101\,ms at P95.
  The mean observed cohort contains 3.65 requests, its P95 is 7, and its maximum
  is 9.

  The controlled benchmark measures the cost of approaching the configured
  bound.
  At $K=64$, median planner latency is 10.99\,ms for mixed cohorts and
  20.10\,ms for completion-oriented cohorts.
  Increasing the cohort to the stress-only value $K=128$ raises these values to
  23.47 and 42.75\,ms, respectively.
  The shaded ranges in Figure~\ref{fig:planner-overhead} are measurement
  dispersion across repetitions, not confidence intervals.

  The gap between the controlled bound and the observed runtime cohorts explains
  why actual passes are much faster than the worst-case benchmark.
  These measurements characterize the evaluated five-replica deployment;
  extrapolating the same latency to substantially larger replica sets requires a
  separate fleet-size study.

  \paragraph{Evaluation takeaway.}
  Together, the experiments show that RAVEL's gains persist across workloads and
  repeated runs, arise from the combination of pre-handoff revisability and
  engine-local protection, and are supported by decision-useful quotes and
  millisecond-scale online planning.
  The evidence characterizes the evaluated five-replica deployment and does not
  constitute a formal SLO guarantee for arbitrary fleet sizes.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM serving engines and phase scheduling.}
Modern LLM serving systems improve execution efficiency through
iteration-level batching and KV-cache management~\cite{orca,vllm},
scalable and parallel inference~\cite{pope_scaling_inference,
deepspeed_inference,alpaserve,loongserve}, optimized attention and
speculative execution~\cite{flashattention2,specinfer}, and structured or
adapter-multiplexed serving~\cite{sglang,slora}.
A complementary line of work explicitly manages the distinct resource demands
of Prefill and Decode.
Sarathi-Serve uses Chunked Prefill to reduce Prefill--Decode interference
~\cite{sarathi}, DistServe disaggregates the two phases and provisions them
against latency objectives~\cite{distserve}, and Splitwise and ShuffleInfer
similarly separate or coordinate phase execution to reduce interference
~\cite{splitwise,shuffleinfer}.
These systems primarily improve how admitted work executes.
RAVEL operates at a different control layer: it determines geographic
placement and preserves bounded placement revisability before engine handoff,
while relying on the local serving engine to execute admitted requests.

\paragraph{Distributed and cache-aware LLM routing.}
Distributed serving systems increasingly route requests using runtime load,
reusable KV state, and adaptive scheduling signals.
Preble jointly considers reusable KV state and computation load
~\cite{preble}, while DualMap balances cache affinity against load and performs
hotspot-aware rebalancing~\cite{dualmap}.
Other work explores learned or adaptive request scheduling
~\cite{llm_learning_to_rank,simple_is_better,lodestar}.
A related body of work improves KV-cache reuse, storage, and transfer through
cache-centric serving and prefix-aware execution
~\cite{mooncake,cachedattention,infinigen,cachecraft,chunkattention,
cacheblend,kvlink}.
These techniques improve destination selection or reduce the cost of executing
a request at a selected destination.
RAVEL does not introduce a new cache primitive or routing score.
Instead, it separates destination selection from commitment:
an arrived request contributes prospective demand to its tentative replica
immediately, while that replica may still change before successful engine
handoff.

\paragraph{Geo-distributed and network-aware placement.}
Recent systems explicitly incorporate geographic and network heterogeneity
into LLM placement.
SkyWalker balances cross-region load while preserving KV-cache locality
~\cite{skylb}; GORGO incorporates WAN latency, queueing state, and
Prefill/cache cost into cross-region decisions~\cite{gorgo}; and Solyx uses
serving telemetry together with WAN measurements for per-request cross-site
placement~\cite{solyx}.
Helix further considers heterogeneous GPU and network resources in LLM serving
~\cite{helix}, while network-aware ML schedulers such as CASSINI coordinate
placement with communication conditions~\cite{cassini}.
More broadly, private-WAN systems such as SWAN and B4 demonstrate the
importance of heterogeneous inter-datacenter paths and traffic constraints
~\cite{swan,b4}.
These systems sharpen the decision of \emph{where} work should execute.
RAVEL makes an additional control decision first-class:
\emph{when should that placement become irreversible?}
It answers this question by retaining Router-owned tentative placement and
prospective accounting until the engine-handoff boundary.

\paragraph{SLO- and application-aware LLM scheduling.}
Prior systems adapt LLM execution and routing to request-specific latency,
deadline, or quality objectives.
JITServe schedules heterogeneous request classes from imprecise request
information~\cite{jitserve}, QUARTZ targets TTFT SLOs through
quantile-aware routing and queueing~\cite{quartz}, Andes considers
quality-of-experience for streamed generation~\cite{andes}, and AdaServe
customizes speculative decoding to per-request SLO requirements
~\cite{adaserve}.
Libra combines request partitioning with SLO-aware scheduling under dynamic
load~\cite{libra}, while complementary work studies serving fairness, SLO
metrics, and serving-performance characterization
~\cite{fairness_llm,revisit_slo_goodput,vidur}.
Application-aware systems exploit structure across related LLM calls:
Parrot coordinates semantic dependencies across requests~\cite{parrot},
Agentix schedules agent programs as first-class execution entities
~\cite{agentix}, and Ayo optimizes end-to-end LLM applications
~\cite{ayo}.
Agent frameworks and benchmarks further expose increasingly multi-stage and
dependency-rich workloads~\cite{autogen,gaia,agentbench,webarena}.
RAVEL applies request-specific SLO semantics at a different control point:
it uses WAN delay and replica serving state to determine which geographic
capacity remains feasible before handoff, while its engine-local controls
preserve the execution assumptions used by those placement decisions.

\paragraph{Reassignment, migration, and online commitment.}
Serving systems can preserve flexibility after an initial assignment through
runtime migration, state movement, preemption, or dynamic orchestration
~\cite{llumnix,spotserve,serverlessllm,dejavu,dlora}.
Llumnix, for example, dynamically reschedules requests across model instances
and uses live migration to improve load balancing and isolation
~\cite{llumnix}.
These mechanisms retain placement flexibility after engine-managed execution
state has materialized.
More broadly, delay scheduling shows that waiting can expose better placement
opportunities~\cite{delayscheduling}, while deadline-aware and online
scheduling systems study finite slack, incomplete information, and scalable
decision making
~\cite{liu_layland_edf,sparrow,flow_imprecise,omega,firmament}.
RAVEL chooses an earlier commitment boundary.
A request may be reassigned while it remains Router-owned, but its placement
becomes final after successful engine handoff, before changing placement would
require moving engine-managed request state.
Pre-handoff replanning and runtime migration are therefore complementary:
the former can avoid some state movement, while the latter can correct
imbalance that emerges after execution begins.

\section{Conclusion}

Geo-distributed LLM serving must route requests online even though regional
capacity is request-dependent: a region that is interchangeable for one
request may be essential for satisfying another request's SLO.
Later arrivals can reveal this opportunity cost, but waiting for such
information consumes the same requests' SLO slack.
RAVEL addresses this tension by separating demand visibility from placement
commitment.
It accounts each request immediately at a tentative replica, while keeping
that placement revisable as long as the request remains Router-owned and
committing it only at successful engine handoff.
Across LMSYS, bursty traffic, and multi-stage DeepResearch workloads, RAVEL
consistently improves request-level SLO attainment while maintaining
competitive latency, with particularly large gains under several
high-contention settings.
More broadly, RAVEL shows that an online serving system need not postpone
demand accounting to preserve the flexibility to exploit information revealed
by later arrivals.

\subsection{Common Request State and Notation}
\label{sec:appendix:state-model}

RAVEL separates \emph{placement ownership} from engine execution state.
Before successful engine handoff, a request is Router-owned: it has one
tentative replica, contributes prospective demand to that replica, and may be
replanned subject to the mobility rules below.  Successful engine handoff
transfers ownership to the serving engine and makes the geographic placement
final.  Engine-side execution may subsequently move through Prefill, Decode,
completion, or failure states, but these events do not reopen geographic
placement.

For request $i$, we use the following notation:
$a_i$ is its Router arrival time; $o_i$ is its client region;
$r_i(t)$ is its tentative replica while Router-owned; $v_i$ is the current
ownership/version counter used by versioned transfers; and $h_i$ identifies the
current engine attempt.  Attempt identifiers isolate asynchronous completion,
cancellation, and cleanup events across retries.

The transport path distinguishes three internal states:
\texttt{WAITING\_MOVABLE}, \texttt{COMMITTING}, and
\texttt{PREFILL\_LOCKED}.  A dispatch claim moves a request from
\texttt{WAITING\_MOVABLE} to the transient \texttt{COMMITTING} state; this is
not a second placement decision.  If target registration fails, Router
ownership is restored.  Placement becomes final only after the target accepts
the request and the engine-facing posting task is created and tracked
successfully.  RAVEL does not perform ordinary geographic rebinds after this
successful handoff.

Across these states, RAVEL enforces five invariants.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single ownership.} Every nonterminal request has exactly one
          control-plane or engine owner.
    \item \textbf{Single tentative placement.} Every Router-owned request is
          attributed to exactly one tentative replica at a time.
    \item \textbf{Exactly-once accounting.} A transferable prospective charge
          is removed from its old owner before being installed at its new
          owner; a request identifier is logically deduplicated when Router,
          pending, and running sets overlap.
    \item \textbf{Attempt isolation.} A callback from attempt $h$ cannot clean
          state belonging to a newer attempt $h'>h$.
    \item \textbf{No post-commit rebind.} Ordinary RAVEL replanning applies
          only before successful handoff; post-handoff imbalance is left to
          the engine or to a separate migration mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Replica SLO Quote Construction}
\label{sec:appendix:quotes}

A placement decision consumes a causal quote
\begin{equation}
    Q_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \left(
        \Phi_{i,r}(t),
        \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}(t),
        \mathbf{z}_{i,r}(t)
    \right),
    \label{eq:appendix:quote-tuple}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{i,r}$ is predicted SLO feasibility,
$\widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}$ is the class-specific ordering objective,
and $\mathbf{z}_{i,r}$ contains secondary capacity-pressure and stability
signals.  Quotes are predictions constructed only from information available
at decision time; they are not formal SLO guarantees.  Their inputs come
from request-visible SLO metadata, directed WAN measurements, calibrated
per-cluster service profiles, currently observed engine state, Router-owned
queue state, and tentative assignments already selected in the same planning
pass.  Shadow prefix locality is used only as a late tie-breaker, and the
evaluated request's realized output length is never used by its quote.

\paragraph{Calibrated service curves.}
For cluster $c$ and active Decode concurrency $n$, the schema-v2 service
profile stores an affine Prefill model
\begin{equation}
    P_{c,n}(p)=b_{c,n}+a_{c,n}p
    \label{eq:appendix:prefill-profile}
\end{equation}
and a measured post-first-token Decode time per token $d_c(n)$.
Profiles are calibrated independently for each serving class and contain an
engine fingerprint.  At runtime RAVEL linearly interpolates between measured
concurrency points and clamps values outside the calibrated concurrency range
to the nearest endpoint rather than extrapolating without bound.  Because
$P_{c,n}$ is measured while $n$ Decode sequences are active, RAVEL does not
add a separate ad-hoc Decode-interference tax to the Prefill estimate.

\paragraph{Queue-ahead Prefill expansion.}
Let $p_i$ be the full prompt-token charge of request $i$.  Without
engine-certified cached-token residency, the Router's shadow prefix estimate
is used only as a locality tie-breaker and does not reduce $p_i$.
For candidate replica $r$, let
$E_r$ denote observed engine-pending Prefill tokens,
$B_{i,r}$ the prompt tokens in Router-owned requests ordered no later than $i$,
$N^{\mathrm{eng}}_r$ the number of engine-pending requests, and
$N^{\mathrm{q}}_{i,r}$ the corresponding number of Router-owned
queue-ahead requests.  With $n_r$ currently running Decode sequences,
RAVEL estimates
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{S}^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,r}
    =
    a_{c(r),n_r}
    \left(E_r+B_{i,r}+p_i\right)
    +
    b_{c(r),n_r}
    \left(N^{\mathrm{eng}}_r+N^{\mathrm{q}}_{i,r}+1\right).
    \label{eq:appendix:prefill-service}
\end{equation}
During cohort replanning, $B_{i,r}$ and $N^{\mathrm{q}}_{i,r}$ additionally
include tentative assignments chosen earlier in the same planning pass.
Thus a later candidate observes the prospective work induced by earlier
choices rather than a stale snapshot.

The point TTFT estimate is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}^{\mathrm{point}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    (t-a_i)
    +
    \rho_{o_i,c(r)}
    +
    \widehat{S}^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,r},
    \label{eq:appendix:ttft-point}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{o_i,c(r)}$ is the directed client-to-cluster WAN delay.
The elapsed term explicitly charges Router observation time against the
request's remaining SLO budget.

\paragraph{Prospective Decode occupancy.}
Let $\mathcal{O}_r$ be the union of request identifiers visible in the
Router-owned queue, engine-pending set, and engine-running set for replica
$r$.  RAVEL forms a prospective sequence count
\begin{equation}
    m_{i,r}
    =
    \max\!\left(
        1,\;
        \left|\mathcal{O}_r\cup\{i\}\right|,\;
        N^{\mathrm{q}}_r+N^{\mathrm{eng}}_r+N^{\mathrm{run}}_r
        +\mathbf{1}[i\notin\mathcal{O}_r]
    \right),
    \label{eq:appendix:prospective-seqs}
\end{equation}
and queries the calibrated Decode curve at $m_{i,r}$, with profile-level
clamping at the measured endpoint.  The set term prevents the same request
from being charged twice when lifecycle views overlap.

\paragraph{LATENCY feasibility.}
For a LATENCY request with TTFT and TBT limits
$D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}}$ and $D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}$,
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{\mathrm{lat}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
       \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}(t)
       \le D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}}
       \;\land\;
       d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r})
       \le D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}
    \right].
    \label{eq:appendix:latency-feasible}
\end{equation}
The two constraints are not combined into a weighted score: a low TTFT cannot
compensate for a predicted TBT violation.

\paragraph{Completion feasibility and output-demand fallback.}
For completion-oriented requests, RAVEL distinguishes request-visible expected
output demand $L_i^{\mathrm{exp}}$ from conservative risk demand
$L_i^{\mathrm{risk}}$:
\begin{align}
    \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{point}}_{i,r}
    &=
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}^{\mathrm{point}}_{i,r}
    +
    \max(0,L_i^{\mathrm{exp}}-1)d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r}),
    \label{eq:appendix:completion-point}\\
    \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{risk}}_{i,r}
    &=
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}^{\mathrm{point}}_{i,r}
    +
    \max(0,L_i^{\mathrm{risk}}-1)d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r}).
    \label{eq:appendix:completion-risk}
\end{align}
Ordinary completion feasibility uses
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{\mathrm{comp}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{risk}}_{i,r}(t)
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{comp}}
    \right],
    \label{eq:appendix:completion-feasible}
\end{equation}
while the expected estimate is used to represent shared service pressure.
For multi-stage COLLECTIVE requests, we use a held-out semantic profile
indexed by $(\textit{stage\_id},\textit{stage\_num})$.  Missing contexts fall
back first to a stage-level aggregate and then to the declared routing hint.
The profile is constructed from training data only; the evaluated request's
realized generation length is never an input to its placement quote.

\paragraph{Residual diagnostics.}
RAVEL records type-specific objective residuals in a bounded history.
For $n$ available residuals and risk level $\epsilon$, the empirical
finite-sample rank is
\begin{equation}
    k
    =
    \left\lceil (n+1)(1-\epsilon) \right\rceil .
    \label{eq:appendix:residual-rank}
\end{equation}
If $k>n$, the quote is marked uncalibrated.  In our evaluation, residuals
are recorded for diagnostics but do not alter placement decisions.  The
reported results therefore do not rely on a cold-start residual guarantee.

The quote procedure is:

\begin{quote}
\textsc{QuoteReplica}$(i,r,t)$:
(1) read calibrated service curves and currently observed engine state;
(2) charge full prompt work and EDF-ordered Router work ahead of $i$;
(3) add tentative work already selected earlier in the current pass;
(4) compute Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:ttft-point};
(5) form the request-ID-deduplicated prospective Decode count in
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:prospective-seqs};
(6) evaluate the LATENCY or completion feasibility predicate;
and (7) return feasibility, the class-specific objective, and secondary
pressure/locality signals.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Prospective Accounting Lifecycle}
\label{sec:appendix:accounting}

RAVEL uses prospective state for two related purposes: to make newly arrived
work visible before commitment and to prevent a planning pass from repeatedly
assigning work against an unchanged snapshot.

\paragraph{Queue and service debt.}
The queue-ahead terms in Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:prefill-service} are derived from
current Router-owned and engine-visible state.  In addition, the WorkYield
planner maintains virtual admission ledgers that make repeated placement
decisions account for already admitted work even when engine lifecycle
feedback has not yet arrived.

For a LATENCY request,
\begin{equation}
    u_{i,r}
    =
    \frac{d_{c(r)}(1)}
         {D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}},
    \qquad
    A_r
    =
    \sum_{j:\,\mathrm{latency\ owner}(j)=r} u_{j,r}.
    \label{eq:appendix:latency-ledger}
\end{equation}
Both numerator and denominator are seconds, so $u_{i,r}$ is dimensionless and
represents one-token-period service demand.  This ledger is a placement
balancing signal rather than a reconstruction of the instantaneous vLLM
queue.

For all requests for which service accounting is active, define
\begin{equation}
    s^{\mathrm{own}}_{i,r}
    =
    \widehat{S}^{\mathrm{own,pre}}_{i,r}
    +
    \widehat{S}^{\mathrm{own,dec}}_{i,r},
    \qquad
    L_r
    =
    \sum_{j:\,\mathrm{service\ owner}(j)=r}
       s^{\mathrm{own}}_{j,r}.
    \label{eq:appendix:service-ledger}
\end{equation}
The Prefill component contains only the request's own profiled service
(including the profile intercept), not queue-ahead debt; the Decode component
is the request-visible expected service used by the corresponding quote.
Thus $L_r$ is measured in seconds of profiled service.

\paragraph{Exactly-once registration and transfer.}
Each persistent virtual charge is accompanied by an owner field on the
request.  Registration is idempotent: if the owner field already exists, the
same request is not added again.  During cohort replanning, RAVEL first removes
the mobile candidates' old charges from the fixed planning base.  It then
processes candidates in deadline order; after each tentative target is chosen,
the request's charge is added to the pass-local target ledger immediately.
After the cohort is complete, persistent ownership is transferred by
subtracting the old charge once and adding the newly computed charge once.
This realizes deferred commitment without deferred accounting.

For completion-oriented accounting, RAVEL explicitly tracks requests carrying a service
virtual owner and removes their active service charge on the matching terminal
callback before requesting another scheduling pass.  The LATENCY token-period
ledger is used as a cumulative placement-balancing signal and is therefore not
interpreted as an exact count of currently running Decode requests; actual
Decode feasibility continues to use the request-ID-deduplicated live
prospective sequence count in Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:prospective-seqs}.

Operationally, arrival installs the request at one tentative owner; a replan
transfers that ownership and its prospective charge exactly once; failed
handoff restores Router ownership; successful handoff makes the placement
final while keeping pending/running engine state visible; and terminal
completion or failure releases the matching active service charge.

The accounting transfer sequence is:

\begin{quote}
\textsc{TransferOrRefresh}$(i,r\!\rightarrow\!r')$:
(1) verify that $i$ is still Router-owned and that its attempt/version is
current;
(2) remove $i$'s old virtual charges from the planning base;
(3) recompute $Q_{i,r'}$ against fixed and pass-local state;
(4) if $r'$ is selected, update the pass-local work/count/service ledgers
immediately;
(5) atomically replace the request's persistent owner/charge fields; and
(6) update the recorded tentative quote.
A failed version/attempt check leaves the current owner unchanged.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Bounded Observation and Replanning}
\label{sec:appendix:replanning}

RAVEL replans from causally observed state rather than polling for an
unbounded future window.  Scheduling operations are serialized by a single
asynchronous scheduler lock.  New arrivals, hold expiration, terminal
feedback, explicit control-plane replan requests, and completion-pressure
changes may request another pass; repeated requests for a pass can be
coalesced before the next serialized execution.

\paragraph{Slack-bounded observation.}
The mobility hold is enabled only for selected non-LATENCY requests.
Let $\delta_i$ be the most recently observed interarrival gap and let
$\mathcal{D}_i$ contain positive RTTs from $i$'s client region to remote
clusters.  The burst predicate is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Burst}_i
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \mathcal{D}_i \neq \emptyset
        \land
        \delta_i \le \max\mathcal{D}_i
    \right].
    \label{eq:appendix:burst-predicate}
\end{equation}

A hold is considered only if the request is predicted feasible in at least two
clusters.  Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{slack}_i
    =
    D_i-\min_r \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}.
    \label{eq:appendix:arrival-slack}
\end{equation}

RAVEL uses the smallest positive remote RTT as the
observation scale and bounds it by available slack:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_i
    =
    \begin{cases}
      \min\!\left(\min\mathcal{D}_i,\mathrm{slack}_i\right),
        & \text{if $i$ is non-LATENCY, $\mathrm{Burst}_i=1$, and}\\
        & \quad \text{$i$ has at least two feasible clusters},\\[1mm]
      0, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:appendix:mobility-hold}
\end{equation}
LATENCY requests are not deliberately held.  They may participate in
pre-handoff reassignment only when a remote SLO-feasible target exists.
The hold changes dispatch eligibility, not accounting: the request is
tentatively placed and visible throughout $[a_i,a_i+\Delta_i)$.

\paragraph{Movable cohort.}
The planner orders movable requests by
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_i
    =
    \left(
       a_i+D_i,\;
       a_i,\;
       id_i
    \right),
    \label{eq:appendix:planning-order}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ denotes the applicable TTFT limit for LATENCY requests and
completion limit otherwise.  A request is excluded if it is no longer
Router-owned, has crossed the handoff boundary, or has exhausted the
one-rebind limit.  The ordinary candidate bound is derived from serving
sequence capacity and is set to \texttt{max\_num\_seqs}.  Under completion
pressure,
Section~\ref{sec:appendix:completion-pressure}, the bound expands to aggregate
sequence capacity across replicas.

\paragraph{Fixed and pass-local context.}
A planning pass freezes the noncandidate engine and Router context.  Fixed
Router requests that precede candidate $i$ under
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:planning-order} contribute their full prompt work and
request intercept to $i$'s quote.  After a target is selected for one mobile
candidate, its prompt work, request count, service charge, and applicable TBT
charge are immediately added to pass-local state before the next candidate is
evaluated.

\begin{quote}
\textsc{Replan}:
(1) collect the bounded movable cohort and sort it by
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:planning-order};
(2) freeze current noncandidate engine/Router state;
(3) remove candidates' old transferable charges from the fixed planning base;
(4) for each candidate in order, quote every legal replica, choose the minimum
lexicographic placement key, and update pass-local state immediately;
(5) apply only still-current assignments, respecting attempt/version and
one-rebind guards; and
(6) dispatch newly eligible work through the ordinary frontier.
\end{quote}

Let $N$ be the movable cohort size, $R$ the number of replicas, and $F$ the
number of fixed Router-owned requests scanned to build queue-ahead prefixes.
The WorkYield planner has planning cost
\begin{equation}
    O(N\log N + F\log F + NR + F),
    \label{eq:appendix:planner-complexity}
\end{equation}
plus constant-size quote interpolation.  This bound is independent of
generated token count; RAVEL does not send one Router scheduling event per
generated token.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Deterministic Placement Key}
\label{sec:appendix:placement-key}

RAVEL avoids combining unlike SLO and load quantities in a fitted weighted
sum.  The WorkYield planner compares candidate replica $r$ for
request $i$ using a lexicographic key whose conceptual order is
\begin{equation}
\small
\begin{split}
    K_{i,r} =
    (&
      \neg\Phi_{i,r},\;
      O_{i,r},\;
      \ell^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r},\;
      \ell^{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r},\\
     &A_r+u_{i,r},\;
      L_r+s^{\mathrm{own}}_{i,r},\;
      \widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}/D_i,\;
      M_{i,r},\;
      \mathrm{moved}_{i,r},\;
      id_r
    ),
    \label{eq:appendix:placement-key}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with lower values preferred.  Terms that are inapplicable to a request class
are set to their neutral value rather than introducing a class-specific
placement algorithm.  The ordering is intentional: feasibility and normalized
SLO violation dominate capacity pressure; capacity pressure dominates
predicted objective and marginal service; movement, prefix locality, and
stable replica identity are used only as late tie-breakers.

The normalized lateness terms are
\begin{align}
    \ell^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}
    &=
    \frac{
       \max(0,\widehat{T}^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}-D_i)
    }{\max(D_i,\varepsilon)},
    \label{eq:appendix:objective-lateness}\\
    \ell^{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}
    &=
    \frac{
       \max(0,\widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}
                 -D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}})
    }{\max(D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}},\varepsilon)}.
    \label{eq:appendix:ttft-lateness}
\end{align}
$O_{i,r}$ is the pending-capacity overflow cost when that guard is applicable.
The convex marginal-service term is
\begin{equation}
    M_{i,r}
    =
    \frac{
       (W_r+s^{\mathrm{own}}_{i,r})^2-W_r^2
    }{D_i^2},
    \label{eq:appendix:marginal-work}
\end{equation}
where $W_r$ is the profiled service already represented at $r$ in the current
planning state.  Equation~\ref{eq:appendix:marginal-work} is the discrete
increment of a quadratic congestion potential, not a learned weight.

Initial single-request placement uses the same safety ordering.  After
feasibility, normalized violation, and virtual-load terms tie, it may consult
current pressure and shadow prefix locality before the stable replica ID.
Prefix locality never precedes feasibility or normalized SLO violation.

\paragraph{Two-request example.}
Suppose request 1 is feasible in $A$ and $B$, while request 2 is feasible only
in $A$.  When request 1 is tentatively assigned during a cohort pass, RAVEL
immediately adds its prompt/service charge to the selected region's pass-local
state.  Request 2 is therefore quoted against that tentative assignment.
If placing request 1 in $A$ makes request 2 infeasible while placing request 1
in $B$ preserves both requests' feasible assignments, the first component of
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:placement-key} dominates later locality or movement
preferences.  This is the concrete mechanism by which request-dependent
regional substitutability affects placement without requiring a fitted
cross-metric score.

\subsection{Completion-Pressure Adaptation}
\label{sec:appendix:completion-pressure}

The completion-pressure path is enabled by request-visible workflow semantics,
not by a dataset name.  A request is considered a workflow-completion request
when it is COLLECTIVE and has more than one stage.  RAVEL tracks the count,
first and latest arrival times, and minimum observed completion SLO among such
arrivals.  It also records whether any nonworkflow traffic has appeared.

Let $C_{\mathrm{seq}}$ be aggregate sequence capacity across replicas and let
$D_{\min}$ be the minimum completion SLO among the observed workflow arrivals.
After a minimum number of samples,
\begin{equation}
    q_{\mathrm{obs}}
    =
    \frac{N_{\mathrm{arr}}-1}
         {t_{\mathrm{last}}-t_{\mathrm{first}}},
    \qquad
    q_{\mathrm{act}}
    =
    \frac{C_{\mathrm{seq}}}
         {\max(1\,\mathrm{s},\,0.5D_{\min})}.
    \label{eq:appendix:completion-pressure-qps}
\end{equation}
Arrival pressure is observed once
$q_{\mathrm{obs}}\ge q_{\mathrm{act}}$.
RAVEL requires
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{arr}}
    \ge
    \max\!\left(
       4,\;
       \min\!\left(16,\left\lfloor
          \frac{C_{\mathrm{seq,replica}}}{4}
       \right\rfloor\right)
    \right)
    \label{eq:appendix:pressure-min-samples}
\end{equation}
before evaluating the rate predicate, where the per-replica quantity is the
configured per-replica pending/sequence limit.
Effective completion pressure additionally requires that workflow-completion
traffic has been observed, no nonworkflow request has been observed, and
outstanding work is nonzero.  There is no separately tuned numerical
hysteresis band: the observed-arrival-pressure bit is causal and sticky within
the run, while the effective pressure path becomes inactive when the
eligibility conditions no longer hold and the active flag is cleared when
outstanding work drains to zero.

Completion pressure changes only two planner inputs.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The movable-candidate ceiling expands from the ordinary bound $K$ to
          aggregate sequence capacity across the deployment.
    \item If the held-out semantic output profile covers the request context,
          completion quotes use its expected output demand rather than the
          ordinary conservative upper estimate for the placement-time expected
          workload.
\end{enumerate}

Completion pressure does not introduce a second Router-side admission queue
or withhold requests predicted to miss their SLOs.  All candidates remain in
the common WorkYield placement path and are dispatched through the CenterYield
frontier.

\paragraph{Semantic output profile.}
The held-out schema-v2 semantic profile indexes
$(\textit{stage\_id},\textit{stage\_num})$.  For covered context $x$ it stores
the training mean and a finite-sample $q_{0.95}$ value.  Lookup follows
\begin{equation}
    x=(\textit{stage\_id},\textit{stage\_num})
    \;\rightarrow\;
    \textit{stage\_id aggregate}
    \;\rightarrow\;
    \text{declared routing hint}.
    \label{eq:appendix:output-profile-fallback}
\end{equation}
Neither the current request's realized output length nor future arrivals enter
this lookup.

When an active completion request terminates, Unified removes its tracked
service virtual charge, deletes the request's service-owner metadata, and
requests another serialized scheduling pass.  Consequently, newly available
completion capacity can influence requests that remain Router-owned.

\subsection{Shallow Reversible Dispatch Frontier}
\label{sec:appendix:frontier}

The CenterYield frontier limits how much Router-visible prompt work can cross
toward engine state at once.  Let
\begin{equation}
    H
    =
    \max_{c\neq c'}
       \rho_{c,c'}
    \label{eq:appendix:control-horizon}
\end{equation}
be the largest configured directed inter-cluster control RTT, and let
$B_{\mathrm{iter}}$ be the engine's
\texttt{max\_num\_batched\_tokens}.  For replica $r$, let
$a_{c(r),0}$ be the calibrated idle/fastest Prefill seconds per token.
RAVEL sets the prompt frontier
\begin{equation}
    F_r
    =
    B_{\mathrm{iter}}
    +
    \left\lceil
       \frac{H}{a_{c(r),0}}
    \right\rceil .
    \label{eq:appendix:frontier-depth}
\end{equation}
Thus the engine can hold approximately one complete scheduling quantum plus
the prompt work that the fastest calibrated Prefill path could consume during
one worst-case control RTT.

At dispatch time, if $E_r$ is currently observed engine-pending prompt work,
the frontier headroom is
\begin{equation}
    G_r
    =
    \max(0,F_r-E_r).
    \label{eq:appendix:frontier-headroom}
\end{equation}
The effective dispatch budget is the minimum of $G_r$ and the replica's
ordinary engine budget.  A Router-owned request may cross the frontier only if
its full conservative prompt charge fits this budget.  Eligible requests are
ordered by their applicable absolute SLO deadline, then arrival time and
request ID.

In our five-replica deployment, the maximum directed RTT is
$H=37.9$\,ms.  We use $B_{\mathrm{iter}}=16384$ tokens,
\texttt{max\_num\_seqs}=64, and 16-token blocks.  The second term of
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix:frontier-depth} is deployment-specific and is recomputed
from the calibrated service profile.

The frontier does not withhold predicted misses in a protected queue.
If an eligible request fits the capacity budget, it is dispatched.  If target
registration rejects the request, the request is restored to Router-owned
state and remains visible to the ordinary replan/dispatch path.  Work that
does not yet fit stays Router-owned, continues to contribute to prospective
planning, and remains movable until successful handoff.

\subsection{Engine-Local TBT Protection}
\label{sec:appendix:tbt-control}

RAVEL's engine adapter protects the local execution assumptions used by
LATENCY quotes.  The adapter is installed only when the target vLLM exposes
the expected scheduler hooks; it fails fast if
\texttt{Scheduler.\_get\_priority} or
\texttt{Scheduler.\_schedule\_chunked\_prefill} is absent rather than silently
running an unvalidated compatibility path.

Let
\begin{equation}
    T
    =
    \min_{i\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{lat}}_r}
       D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}
    \label{eq:appendix:tightest-tbt}
\end{equation}
be the tightest active TBT among LATENCY Decode requests at replica $r$.
From the bound profile, the adapter takes
\begin{equation}
    a^{\mathrm{guard}}_r = \max_n a_{c(r),n},
    \qquad
    b^{\mathrm{guard}}_r = \max_n b_{c(r),n}.
    \label{eq:appendix:prefill-guard-envelope}
\end{equation}
For block size $g$ and ordinary token limit $B_{\max}$, it computes
\begin{align}
    q_{\mathrm{raw}}
      &=
      \left\lfloor
         \frac{\max(0,T-b^{\mathrm{guard}}_r)}
              {a^{\mathrm{guard}}_r}
      \right\rfloor,
      \label{eq:appendix:chunk-raw}\\
    q_{\mathrm{blk}}
      &=
      \min\!\left(
        B_{\max},
        \max\!\left(
          g,\;
          g\left\lfloor\frac{q_{\mathrm{raw}}}{g}\right\rfloor
        \right)
      \right).
      \label{eq:appendix:chunk-aligned}
\end{align}
If $n^{\mathrm{dec}}_r$ Decode sequences are active, the temporary
block-aligned batch budget is
\begin{equation}
    B^{\mathrm{guard}}_r
    =
    g
    \left\lceil
       \frac{n^{\mathrm{dec}}_r+q_{\mathrm{blk}}}{g}
    \right\rceil .
    \label{eq:appendix:guard-batch-budget}
\end{equation}
The adapter sets
\begin{equation}
    B_{\mathrm{iter}}
    \leftarrow
    \min(B_{\max},B^{\mathrm{guard}}_r)
    \label{eq:appendix:temporary-batch-cap}
\end{equation}
for that Chunked-Prefill scheduling call only and restores $B_{\max}$ in a
\texttt{finally} block.

If no active Decode carries a LATENCY TBT obligation, the adapter follows the
ordinary vLLM scheduling path.  If even one minimum-size block exceeds the
profiled TBT budget, the adapter must still provide vLLM a legal block and
therefore cannot claim a TBT guarantee in that physical regime; the Router's
replica-feasibility test is intended to avoid such placements, and any
realized violation remains in the evaluation denominator.

Phase-aware priority metadata carries a remaining deadline budget rather than
a Router monotonic-clock timestamp.  The engine reconstructs a local deadline
from its own arrival clock, avoiding an implicit cross-host monotonic-clock
synchronization assumption.  Non-RAVEL priorities occupy a different range,
so the adapter remains inert for ordinary vLLM work.

\subsection{Commitment and Engine-Handoff State Machine}
\label{sec:appendix:handoff}

RAVEL defines commitment as a successful ownership transition, not as a timer
expiration or queue removal.  The Router may choose a tentative target, hold a
request, replan it, and mark it dispatch-eligible without making its placement
final.

The engine-facing handoff path is:
(1) freeze the latest Router quote for observability;
(2) ask the target replica to register the request;
(3) create the asynchronous engine-facing HTTP posting task; and
(4) install that task in the attempt-indexed task table.
The quote-freeze callback is diagnostic and occurs before registration; it
does not itself commit placement.  Successful registration together with
successful task creation/tracking is the paper-level commitment boundary.
Before that point, a dispatch claim may either succeed or roll back: target
registration rejection restores the Router-owned request, and setup failure
cleans the matching partial attempt.  After successful handoff, completion,
failure, and cancellation perform attempt-specific cleanup but do not reopen
geographic placement.  Callbacks whose attempt identifier no longer matches
the active attempt are ignored.

Task cleanup is attempt-aware: a deletion request carrying attempt $h$ is
ignored when the stored task belongs to a different attempt.  The same
attempt identifier is passed through completion/failure callbacks, preventing
a delayed callback from an earlier posting attempt from releasing state
belonging to a newer one.

Before successful handoff, RAVEL changes only Router/transport metadata and
prospective accounting; the serving engine has not yet established the
engine-managed execution state whose migration would be required after
execution starts.  RAVEL therefore obtains pre-handoff placement flexibility
without KV-state migration.  Once handoff succeeds, later pending/running
engine state remains visible to subsequent quotes, but the committed request
itself is no longer an ordinary geographic-replanning candidate.

\begin{quote}
A request remains reassignable while it is Router-owned.
Its placement becomes final only after the target replica has accepted
registration and the corresponding engine-facing posting task has been
created and tracked successfully.
\end{quote}

\section{Repeated-Run Robustness}
\label{app:repeated-runs}

This appendix reports a five-run paired robustness study at
$16\times$ load.  The repeated-run statistics are reported separately from
single-run headline results because they summarize different experimental
repetitions.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Result Context and Comparator Definitions}
\label{sec:appendix:result-contexts}

For DeepResearch at $16\times$ load, the single-run comparison in the main
evaluation reports 79.0\% request-level SLO attainment for \textsc{RAVEL} and
65.6\% for DualMap, a gain of 13.4 percentage points.  The repeated-run study
uses DualMap as the fixed comparator for all three workloads and all five
repetitions.

The five-run DeepResearch study yields a mean paired gain of 17.40 percentage
points over DualMap.  This value is reported separately from the 13.4
percentage-point single-run result because the two statistics summarize
different experimental repetitions.


% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Five-Run Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:appendix:repeated-run-protocol}

For each workload
$w\in\{\mathrm{LMSYS},\mathrm{Burst},\mathrm{DeepResearch}\}$ at
$16\times$ load, the robustness experiment consists of five complete paired
replays.  Within paired repetition $j$, RAVEL and the workload's fixed
comparator use the same request set and order, trace timing, random seed,
serving endpoints, physical-WAN topology, model/engine configuration, cache
reset procedure, and warm-up protocol.

For workload $w$ and paired run $j$, define the SLO-attainment gain
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{w,j}
    =
    \mathrm{SLOAttain}_{\textsc{RAVEL},w,j}
    -
    \mathrm{SLOAttain}_{\mathrm{DualMap},w,j}.
    \label{eq:appendix:paired-gain}
\end{equation}
The five-run paired mean is
\begin{equation}
    \overline{\Delta}_w
    =
    \frac{1}{5}
    \sum_{j=1}^{5}
    \Delta_{w,j}.
    \label{eq:appendix:mean-paired-gain}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
    s_{\Delta,w}
    =
    \sqrt{
        \frac{1}{4}
        \sum_{j=1}^{5}
        \left(
            \Delta_{w,j}-\overline{\Delta}_w
        \right)^2
    }.
    \label{eq:appendix:paired-std}
\end{equation}
We compute a two-sided 95\% Student-$t$ confidence interval over the five
paired gains:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{\Delta}_w
    \;\pm\;
    t_{0.975,4}
    \frac{s_{\Delta,w}}{\sqrt{5}},
    \qquad
    t_{0.975,4}=2.776.
    \label{eq:appendix:paired-ci}
\end{equation}
The pairing is performed at the replay level; we do not construct independent
confidence intervals for RAVEL and the comparator and subtract them.  The five
paired gains (in percentage points) are
LMSYS: $\{3.2,2.4,8.0,0.8,6.0\}$,
Burst: $\{2.4,7.2,9.2,9.4,9.2\}$, and
DeepResearch: $\{19.4,18.6,11.8,25.0,12.2\}$.

The repeated-run figure plots one point per workload at
