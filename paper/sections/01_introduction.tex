\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Geo-distributed LLM serving expands available GPU capacity, but not every
region is equally useful to every request.
Large-scale private WANs expose heterogeneous inter-region paths
~\cite{b4,swan}, while distributed inference systems must additionally contend
with heterogeneous accelerator, serving, and network conditions
~\cite{helix,cassini,skylb,gorgo,solyx}.
Together with heterogeneous request-level SLOs, these differences make
placement feasibility request-specific: a region that is optional for one
request may be the only SLO-feasible choice for another.
Requests may impose different TTFT, TBT, or end-to-end completion objectives
~\cite{distserve,jitserve,quartz,andes,revisit_slo_goodput}.
Regional capacity therefore has request-dependent opportunity cost: assigning
a region to one request may consume capacity that another request cannot
substitute away from.

This asymmetry makes an otherwise reasonable placement costly when later demand
arrives.
Existing distributed routers increasingly improve \emph{where} an arriving
request should execute using serving load, cache locality, WAN conditions,
request-level SLOs, and adaptive feedback
~\cite{preble,dualmap,skylb,gorgo,quartz,lodestar}.
However, they largely couple destination selection with commitment: once a
request is placed using the currently visible state, later arrivals generally
cannot change that placement before execution begins.
Consider two regions, $A$ and $B$.
Request $r_1$ can satisfy its SLO in either region, whereas a later request
$r_2$ can succeed only in $A$.
Placing $r_1$ in the currently faster $A$ is locally reasonable, but consumes
$r_2$'s only feasible region; redirecting $r_1$ to $B$ allows both requests to
succeed.
This opportunity also appears in execution traces.
In a trace-level decomposition of the Burst $12\times$ mechanism run, our
conservative criterion confirms pre-handoff rescue events for at least
41.25\% of misses under frozen placement: same-plan reassignment makes the
affected request predicted feasible, and the request subsequently meets its
SLO.
The missing question is therefore not only
\emph{where should this request execute?}, but also
\emph{when should an earlier placement become irreversible?}

A natural response is to delay commitment so that later arrivals can influence
placement.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(a) shows that fixed-delay late binding initially
helps: short deferral exposes additional demand and improves SLO attainment.
The benefit is not monotonic, however, and eventually reverses; at
$\tau=80$~ms, SLO attainment falls to 54.2\%.
Thus, preserving placement flexibility for longer is not by itself sufficient.

The dominant recoverable loss comes from delaying demand visibility rather than
from the direct SLO-budget cost of waiting.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(b) isolates these effects at the same
$\tau=80$~ms operating point.
An oracle that removes the artificial hold from SLO accounting while preserving
the same late-binding behavior raises attainment only from 54.2\% to 54.8\%,
a 0.6-percentage-point recovery.
By contrast, preserving the same 80-ms final-binding delay while making held
requests immediately visible to prospective capacity accounting raises
attainment to 69.2\%, recovering 15.0 percentage points.
Fixed-delay late binding therefore preserves useful placement flexibility while
withholding already-arrived demand from subsequent planning, leaving later
requests with an incomplete view of prospective replica load.
The dominant failure is thus not delay alone, but delaying demand visibility
together with commitment.

RAVEL's key insight is that deferred commitment does not require deferred
accounting.
A request can influence capacity planning from the moment it arrives while its
tentative placement remains revisable until engine handoff.
On arrival, RAVEL obtains replica-specific SLO quotes, selects a tentative
serving replica, and immediately attributes the request's prospective demand
to that replica.
While the request remains Router-owned, newly revealed demand or serving-state
changes may trigger bounded replanning; reassignment transfers both tentative
ownership and its prospective charge.
Only successful engine handoff makes placement final.
This lifecycle keeps tentative demand visible throughout while preserving the
ability to correct stale pre-handoff allocations.

Turning this separation into a practical serving system requires four
capabilities.
First, the Router must determine which replicas remain SLO-feasible under
heterogeneous WAN delay, engine backlog, Prefill--Decode interference, and
request-specific success criteria.
Second, tentative work must remain visible exactly once as placement changes,
requiring prospective accounting that moves atomically with ownership.
Third, newly observed information must be exploited without unbounded waiting
or repeatedly reconsidering all outstanding work, requiring bounded
replanning.
Finally, commitment and local execution must preserve the feasibility
assumptions used by geographic placement while handling handoff failures
safely.
RAVEL realizes these requirements with calibrated replica-specific SLO quotes,
transferable prospective accounting, a bounded Router-owned replanning cohort,
an explicit engine-handoff commitment boundary, and engine-local TBT
protection.

We implement RAVEL as an asynchronous routing layer for vLLM~\cite{vllm} and
evaluate it on five serving replicas across three clusters connected by
physical WAN paths.
At $16\times$ load, RAVEL improves request-level SLO attainment over DualMap by
\textbf{8.8}, \textbf{11.2}, and \textbf{13.4 percentage points} on LMSYS,
Burst, and DeepResearch, respectively, while reducing P95 TTFT by
\textbf{49.1--80.5\%} and P95 end-to-end latency by
\textbf{15.5--56.8\%}.
Mechanism ablations reinforce the same design logic: removing prospective
accounting lowers SLO attainment by \textbf{21.4 percentage points}, disabling
pre-handoff reassignment lowers it by \textbf{13.2 points}, and removing
engine-local TBT protection lowers it by \textbf{9.8 points}.
Online replanning remains millisecond-scale, with a measured P95
scheduling-pass latency of 2.10\,ms on Burst $12\times$.

In summary, this paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
    We identify the request-dependent opportunity cost of regional capacity and
    show that premature commitment creates avoidable allocation-induced SLO
    misses.
    We further diagnose fixed-delay late binding and find that its dominant
    recoverable loss comes from delaying already-arrived demand visibility
    rather than from the direct waiting penalty.

    \item
    We introduce an immediate-accounting, deferred-commitment abstraction:
    tentative demand influences capacity planning from arrival while placement
    remains revisable until engine handoff.
    RAVEL realizes this abstraction through replica-specific SLO quotes,
    transferable prospective accounting, bounded pre-handoff replanning, and a
    failure-safe commitment boundary.

    \item
    We implement RAVEL on vLLM and evaluate it on a physical-WAN deployment,
    demonstrating higher request-level SLO attainment across conversational,
    bursty, and completion-oriented workloads with millisecond-scale online
    planning overhead.
\end{itemize}
