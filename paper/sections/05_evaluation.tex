\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate RAVEL along five dimensions.
\textbf{RQ1:} Does RAVEL improve request-level SLO attainment as offered load
increases?
\textbf{RQ2:} Do these gains persist across repeated runs?
\textbf{RQ3:} How much do immediate prospective accounting, pre-handoff
revisability, and engine-local TBT protection each contribute to the
end-to-end gains?
\textbf{RQ4:} Are replica-specific quotes accurate enough to support
request-level feasibility decisions?
\textbf{RQ5:} Is bounded replanning fast enough for the online serving path?

The main result matrix reports one 500-request replay per
policy--workload--load cell.
A separate five-run campaign evaluates replay-to-replay variation at
$16\times$ load.
We report these two experimental contexts separately and do not average their
results together.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval:setup}

\paragraph{Testbed.}
We evaluate all policies on the same five-replica, three-cluster
physical-WAN deployment.
The replicas are distributed across the clusters in a $2/2/1$ configuration.
Each replica serves Qwen3-1.7B on one NVIDIA RTX~4090 using
vLLM~0.8.5.post1's V0 scheduler with bfloat16 weights.
The directed physical WAN paths have measured RTTs ranging from
10.1 to 37.9\,ms.
Table~\ref{tab:eval-setup} summarizes the common engine and replay settings.

\paragraph{Workloads and SLOs.}
The three workloads stress different sources of online placement uncertainty.
\emph{LMSYS} represents heterogeneous conversational traffic.
\emph{Burst} preserves the request contents of its source trace but replaces
arrival timestamps with a bursty schedule, stressing decisions made from
rapidly changing queue state.
\emph{DeepResearch} represents multi-stage completion workflows whose output
demand depends on request-visible stage metadata.

Each request carries an SLO class and a request-specific multiplier.
The base budgets are 0.8\,s for TTFT, 80\,ms for TBT, and 8\,s for completion
latency, after which the configured multiplier is applied.
A LATENCY request succeeds only if it satisfies both its TTFT bound and every
TBT bound.
THROUGHPUT and COLLECTIVE requests succeed if their end-to-end completion
latency remains within the corresponding completion budget.

\paragraph{Replay protocol.}
We vary contention by compressing the original inter-arrival timeline while
preserving request order, contents, output metadata, and SLOs.
At load factor $x$, every original inter-arrival interval is divided by $x$.
Before each cell, we reset replica prefix caches and start a fresh policy run.
All policies receive the same request-visible information and run under the
same model, topology, WAN paths, and replay schedule.
The main matrix contains one 500-request replay per cell.
The repeated-run campaign is conducted separately using five paired replays at
$16\times$ load.

\paragraph{Policies and metrics.}
We compare RAVEL with RoundRobin (RR), LeastLoad (LL), RD, DualMap, and
SkyWalker.
For the single-run headline results, numerical gains are reported relative to
DualMap.
The repeated-run campaign follows its designated best-baseline protocol.

Our primary metric is request-level SLO attainment.
Secondary metrics are time to first token (TTFT), end-to-end latency (E2E), and
time per output token (TPOT).
TTFT and E2E are measured from request arrival at the Router and therefore
include Router-owned time, WAN delay, queueing, and engine execution.


\subsection{End-to-End Performance}
\label{sec:eval:e2e}

RAVEL's advantage grows with contention, when decisions based only on
arrival-time state are more likely to become stale before execution.
Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} shows that RAVEL maintains the highest
request-level SLO attainment across the evaluated load sweep.
The separation becomes most visible at high load, where rapidly changing
prospective queue state increases the cost of committing a geographic
placement too early.

At $16\times$ load, RAVEL improves SLO attainment over DualMap by
\textbf{8.8 percentage points} on LMSYS,
\textbf{11.2 points} on Burst, and
\textbf{13.4 points} on DeepResearch.
The corresponding P95 TTFT reductions range from
\textbf{49.1--80.5\%}, while P95 E2E reductions range from
\textbf{15.5--56.8\%}.
These results show that retaining pre-handoff flexibility does not require
sacrificing tail latency to obtain higher request-level SLO attainment.

The $1\times$ distributions in Figure~\ref{fig:main-results} serve a different
purpose from the load sweep: they isolate latency behavior when capacity is
comparatively plentiful.
Even in this low-contention regime, RAVEL does not introduce a large latency
penalty from maintaining tentative placements.
More broadly, the overlapping latency distributions also illustrate why raw
latency alone is not the serving objective.
A request with sufficient slack may tolerate a different placement if doing so
preserves scarce SLO-feasible capacity for a more constrained request.
RAVEL therefore optimizes the fraction of requests satisfying their own SLOs,
rather than minimizing every latency sample independently.


\subsection{Repeated-Run Robustness}
\label{sec:eval:robustness}

The load sweep establishes behavior across workloads and contention levels,
while a separate paired campaign tests replay-to-replay robustness at
$16\times$ load.
For each workload, every run contains a complete 500-request replay, and the
RAVEL result is paired with the designated best baseline under the same run
conditions.
Each paired replay contributes one difference in request-level SLO attainment;
individual requests within a replay are not treated as independent statistical
samples.

The repeated runs preserve RAVEL's advantage on all three workloads.
Mean gains are 4.1 percentage points on LMSYS, 7.5 points on Burst, and
17.4 points on DeepResearch.
For each workload, the 95\% confidence interval over the five paired run-level
differences lies above zero.
This supports the conclusion that the average improvement persists under
replay variation, without implying that every request or every possible replay
must improve.

These five-run values are separate from the single-run matrix in
Figure~\ref{fig:main-results}.
They come from a different experimental batch and use a different statistical
unit.
For example, the 17.4-point DeepResearch repeated-run mean should not be
substituted for the 13.4-point single-run headline result.
Appendix~\ref{app:repeated-runs} reports the individual paired values and
statistical construction.

\subsection{Mechanism Contributions}
\label{sec:eval:mechanism}

We isolate RAVEL's three main mechanisms on Burst at $12\times$, where
burst-driven state changes are pronounced without the system collapsing into
universal overload.
\emph{NoAccounting} retains replica-specific SLO quotes, pre-handoff
replanning, and engine-local TBT protection, but excludes Router-owned
tentative demand from the prospective state used by subsequent placement
decisions.
\emph{FixedPlacement} retains RAVEL's initial SLO-aware placement,
prospective accounting, and TBT guard, but disables pre-handoff reassignment.
\emph{NoTBTGuard} retains global routing, prospective accounting, and
replanning, but disables the engine-local Prefill control used to protect
active LATENCY Decode requests.



Removing prospective accounting causes the largest attainment drop.
Full RAVEL attains 81.2\% request-level SLO attainment, compared with
59.8\% for NoAccounting
(\textbf{$-21.4$ percentage points}),
68.0\% for FixedPlacement
(\textbf{$-13.2$ points}), and
71.4\% for NoTBTGuard
(\textbf{$-9.8$ points}).
The NoAccounting result closes the loop with
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}: allowing placement to remain revisable is not
sufficient if tentative work disappears from the state used to make subsequent
capacity decisions.
Likewise, the FixedPlacement gap shows that immediate accounting alone cannot
repair a stale geographic assignment once that assignment has been made
irreversible.

The component-level metrics show that the three mechanisms protect different
failure surfaces.
NoAccounting increases P95 TTFT from 2.905\,s to 3.641\,s, raises the
LATENCY-request TBT-violation rate from 6.6\% to 19.3\%, and increases P95 E2E
from 18.985\,s to 20.928\,s.
FixedPlacement obtains a lower P95 TTFT than RAVEL
(2.490 versus 2.905\,s), yet its TBT-violation rate rises to 37.6\% and its
P95 E2E increases to 21.347\,s.
NoTBTGuard reduces P95 TTFT further to 1.951\,s and has nearly the same P95 E2E
as RAVEL (18.958 versus 18.985\,s), but its TBT-violation rate reaches 42.0\%.
Thus, low TTFT or E2E alone does not imply high request-level SLO attainment:
prospective accounting, geographic revisability, and local TBT protection
address complementary sources of failure.

A trace-level decomposition of the same Burst $12\times$ mechanism run
explains why pre-handoff reassignment is useful.
We conservatively classify a miss under frozen pre-handoff placement as a
confirmed rescue only when the same planning episode makes the affected
request change from predicted infeasible to predicted feasible through
reassignment, and that request subsequently satisfies its realized SLO.
Under this criterion, at least \textbf{41.25\%} of frozen-placement misses are
confirmed rescue events without adding serving capacity.
The remaining misses are unresolved by this criterion rather than proven
irrecoverable.
This statistic is a trace-level decomposition of the same mechanism run, not a
separate experimental campaign.


\subsection{Quote Accuracy}
\label{sec:eval:quote}

Replica-specific quotes are useful only if they preserve the feasibility
decision that drives placement and replanning.
Across the selected final-handoff records in our $16\times$ quote analysis,
the empirical false-feasible and false-infeasible rates are
\textbf{6.2\%} and \textbf{1.0\%}, respectively.

For final-handoff record $i$, we define normalized quote error as
\[
    e_i =
    \frac{\widehat{L}_i-L_i}{B_i},
\]
where $\widehat{L}_i$ is predicted latency, $L_i$ is observed latency, and
$B_i$ is the corresponding request-specific SLO budget.
For LATENCY requests, $L_i$ denotes TTFT; for completion-oriented requests,
$L_i$ denotes E2E.
Negative error is optimistic and positive error is conservative.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/quote_accuracy_figure.pdf}
    \caption{
    \textbf{Quote accuracy in the $16\times$ quote analysis.}
    (a) Empirical CDF of normalized quote error
    $(\widehat{L}_i-L_i)/B_i$ for LATENCY (TTFT) and completion (E2E) requests;
    shading marks errors within $\pm10\%$ of the applicable SLO budget.
    (b) Feasibility-classification error rates over the same
    $N=2{,}500$ selected final-handoff records.
    False-feasible and false-infeasible rates are 6.2\% and 1.0\%,
    respectively.
    }
    \label{fig:quote-accuracy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:quote-accuracy} shows that most normalized errors remain near
zero, although completion quotes exhibit a longer optimistic tail.
More importantly, the quotes usually preserve the decision-relevant
feasibility classification.
Of the 1,672 records quoted as feasible, 104 later violate the corresponding
SLO, yielding a 6.2\% empirical false-feasible rate.
Of the 828 records quoted as infeasible, only 8 complete feasibly, yielding a
1.0\% empirical false-infeasible rate.

These measurements cover placements that RAVEL actually selected and handed
off; they do not observe counterfactual execution on unselected replicas.
The reported rates therefore characterize the empirical quality of the online
decision signal rather than providing a formal guarantee that every feasible
quote will satisfy its SLO.


\subsection{Planner Overhead}
\label{sec:eval:overhead}

Bounded replanning adds millisecond-scale latency on the actual online control
path.
Figure~\ref{fig:planner-overhead}(b) reports scheduling-pass latency from the
Burst $12\times$ mechanism run.
Across 281 actual passes, median latency is 0.998\,ms and P95 latency is
2.101\,ms.
The mean observed cohort contains 3.65 requests, its P95 is 7, and its maximum
is 9, well below the configured online bound of $K=64$.


A controlled benchmark measures the cost of approaching the configured cohort
bound.
At $K=64$, median planner latency is 10.99\,ms for mixed cohorts and
20.10\,ms for completion-oriented cohorts.
Increasing the cohort to the stress-only value $K=128$ raises these values to
23.47 and 42.75\,ms, respectively.
The shaded ranges in Figure~\ref{fig:planner-overhead} show measurement
dispersion across repetitions rather than confidence intervals.

The gap between the controlled bound and the much smaller cohorts observed at
runtime explains why actual scheduling passes are substantially faster than
the bound-oriented benchmark.
These measurements characterize the evaluated five-replica deployment;
extrapolating the same control latency to substantially larger replica sets
requires a separate fleet-size study.

\paragraph{Evaluation takeaway.}
Together, the experiments show that RAVEL's gains persist across workloads and
repeated runs and arise from three complementary mechanisms:
immediate prospective accounting keeps uncommitted demand visible,
pre-handoff replanning corrects stale allocations, and engine-local TBT
protection preserves the feasibility assumptions used by the Router.
Replica-specific quotes provide a decision-useful feasibility signal, while
bounded replanning remains millisecond-scale on the evaluated online path.
