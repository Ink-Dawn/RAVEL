\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:design:overview}

RAVEL separates when an arrived request begins influencing capacity planning
from when its placement becomes final.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ravel-overview}, an arrival is first evaluated
against the available replicas, tentatively assigned to one replica, and
immediately incorporated into RAVEL's prospective demand state.
While the request remains router-owned, later arrivals may trigger bounded
replanning of that assignment.
Successful handoff to the selected serving engine makes the placement final;
pending and running engine state then continues to inform later decisions.

The design follows directly from the requirements in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}.
Replica SLO quotes determine request-specific feasibility
(Section~\ref{sec:design:feasibility});
prospective accounting makes tentative demand visible before commitment
(Section~\ref{sec:design:accounting});
bounded replanning preserves controlled pre-handoff flexibility
(Section~\ref{sec:design:replanning});
and SLO-specific placement and admission allocate scarce serving capacity
before the handoff boundary
(Sections~\ref{sec:design:policy} and~\ref{sec:design:commitment}).


\subsection{Replica SLO Quotes}
\label{sec:design:feasibility}

A single load score is insufficient under heterogeneous SLOs.
A lightly loaded replica may still be infeasible once WAN delay and remaining
slack are considered, and lower TTFT cannot compensate for violating a hard
TBT constraint.
RAVEL therefore separates \emph{estimating} a request--replica assignment
from \emph{choosing} that assignment.
Let $c\in\mathcal C$ denote a serving cluster,
$\mathcal R_c$ its replicas, and $c(r)$ the cluster containing replica $r$.
For request $i$ and candidate replica $r$, a
\emph{replica SLO quote} predicts the SLO-relevant cost and feasibility of
assigning $i$ to $r$ from calibrated service profiles, WAN RTT, live engine
state, router-owned work, and elapsed time.

At active Decode concurrency $n$, RAVEL models Prefill service as
$P_{c,n}(p)=b_{c,n}+a_{c,n}p$ and denotes calibrated post-first-token Decode
time per token by $d_c(n)$.
Let $\widehat S^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,r}$ denote the predicted Prefill-side
service before $i$ receives its first token.
It includes observed engine-side Prefill debt, router-owned prompt work ahead
of $i$, and $i$'s own prompt work; tentative assignments made earlier in the
same planning pass enter this state immediately.
RAVEL predicts
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    (t-a_i)
    +
    \rho_{o_i,c(r)}
    +
    \widehat S^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,r},
    \label{eq:ravel-ttft}
\end{equation}
where $a_i$ is the arrival time and $\rho_{o_i,c(r)}$ is the RTT from the
request origin to cluster $c(r)$.
The elapsed term makes feasibility explicitly slack-dependent: a replica that
is feasible at arrival can cease to be feasible while the request waits.

For Decode, RAVEL uses a conservative prospective sequence count
$m_{i,r}$ over router-owned, engine-pending, and running requests, logically
deduplicating request IDs.
For a LATENCY request with TTFT and TBT bounds
$D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}}$ and $D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}$, replica $r$ is feasible iff
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{\mathrm{lat}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}(t)
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{TTFT}}
        \;\land\;
        d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r})
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}
    \right].
    \label{eq:ravel-latency-feasible}
\end{equation}
RAVEL keeps TTFT and TBT as separate success constraints.
A normalized scalar incorporating Decode pressure is used only to order
otherwise comparable placements and never replaces
Equation~\ref{eq:ravel-latency-feasible}.

Completion-oriented requests instead require total-service estimates.
RAVEL distinguishes request-visible expected and conservative output demands,
$L_i^{\mathrm{exp}}$ and $L_i^{\mathrm{risk}}$:
\begin{align}
    \widehat T^{\mathrm{point}}_{i,r}
    &=
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}
    +(L_i^{\mathrm{exp}}-1)d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r}),
    \nonumber\\
    \widehat T^{\mathrm{risk}}_{i,r}
    &=
    \widehat{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{i,r}
    +(L_i^{\mathrm{risk}}-1)d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r}).
    \label{eq:ravel-completion-estimates}
\end{align}
Outside completion soft admission, feasibility uses the conservative estimate:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi^{\mathrm{comp}}_{i,r}(t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
        \widehat T^{\mathrm{risk}}_{i,r}(t)
        \le D_i^{\mathrm{comp}}
    \right].
    \label{eq:ravel-completion-feasible}
\end{equation}
During completion soft admission, RAVEL may use an expected-output profile
constructed offline from held-out data and indexed only by request-visible
workflow metadata.
The evaluated request's realized generation length is never exposed to the
Router.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:quotes} gives the full Prefill expansion,
profile construction, prospective-count semantics, and ordering objectives.


\subsection{Prospective Regional Demand Accounting}
\label{sec:design:accounting}

Later arrivals can exploit tentative placements only if already-arrived work
is visible before commitment.
Waiting until handoff to account for that work would hide current demand,
whereas committing merely to expose it would remove the flexibility RAVEL
seeks to preserve.
RAVEL therefore combines
\emph{queue-based accounting}, which captures router-side Prefill queueing,
with \emph{virtual accounting}, which captures replica-dependent service
pressure not represented by queue position alone.

When request $i$ is tentatively assigned to replica $r$, it belongs to $r$'s
router-owned queue and its full prompt work immediately contributes to later
quotes.
Earlier tentative assignments within the same cohort likewise update the
planning state seen by later candidates.
Each router-owned request belongs to exactly one replica queue, preventing its
prompt work from being represented at multiple tentative destinations.

    \centering
    \small
    \caption{Prospective planning state used by RAVEL.}
    \label{tab:ravel-demand-state}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
        \toprule
        Signal & Per-request & Aggregate & Role \\
        \midrule
        Prefill & $p_i$ & $W^{\mathrm{ahead}}_{i,r}$ & Queueing \\
        Service & $s^{\mathrm{own}}_{i,r}$ & $L_r$ & Service balance \\
        TBT & $u_{i,r}$ & $A_r$ & Admission balance \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Prompt work alone does not capture heterogeneous service demand.
RAVEL therefore associates request $i$ with a replica-dependent own-service
charge
\begin{equation}
    s^{\mathrm{own}}_{i,r}
    =
    b_{c(r),n_r}
    +a_{c(r),n_r}p_i
    +
    \begin{cases}
        (L_i^{\mathrm{exp}}-1)d_{c(r)}(m_{i,r}),
            & \text{completion},\\
        0,   & \text{LATENCY}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:ravel-own-service}
\end{equation}
This charge excludes engine backlog and queue-ahead work and represents only
the profiled service induced by $i$.
RAVEL accumulates these charges in $L_r$, an active-request virtual service
load.
The charge transfers with reassignment, persists through engine handoff, and
is released at terminal completion.

LATENCY requests additionally contribute
$u_{i,r}=d_{c(r)}(1)/D_i^{\mathrm{TBT}}$ to a lightweight per-replica
TBT-admission balancing signal $A_r$.
$A_r$ is cumulative over the scheduler instance and transfers with
reassignment rather than request completion; it is distinct from live Decode
occupancy represented by $m_{i,r}$ and engine state.

Accounting follows tentative ownership.
If request $i$ moves from replica $r$ to $r'$, an applicable virtual ledger
$Z$ is updated conceptually as
\[
    Z_r \leftarrow Z_r-z_{i,r},
    \qquad
    Z_{r'} \leftarrow Z_{r'}+z_{i,r'},
\]
with the destination charge recomputed for $r'$.
Router-queue ownership moves with the request as well.
Reassignment transfers ownership rather than preserving a physical queue
position: at the target replica, the request participates in the normal
planning-deadline and SLO-aware dispatch order.
Thus, prospective demand follows tentative placement without making that
placement final.


\subsection{Bounded Replanning}
\label{sec:design:replanning}

Continuously reconsidering every outstanding request would expose the largest
possible reassignment set, but would also increase control-plane cost and
placement churn while SLO slack is being consumed.
RAVEL instead replans event-by-event over a bounded movable cohort.
Only router-owned requests may change placement; engine-owned work is fixed
but continues to influence replica quotes.

Replanning triggers and mobility eligibility are distinct.
LATENCY arrivals request an immediate pass, although a LATENCY request itself
is mobile only when another cluster contains an SLO-feasible replica.
A non-LATENCY arrival with no mobility hold also triggers replanning;
otherwise its hold expiration does.
Release of active virtual service and completion soft-admission scheduling can
likewise request another pass.

For selected non-LATENCY requests, RAVEL exposes a short
\emph{mobility hold}.
RAVEL declares an \emph{RTT-scale burst} when the latest observed
inter-arrival gap is no larger than the largest positive RTT from the request
origin to a remote serving cluster.
For request $i$, define arrival slack as
\[
    \sigma_i^{\mathrm{arr}}
    =
    \max\!\left\{
        0,\,
        \Delta_i
        -
        \min_r \widehat T^{\mathrm{obj}}_{i,r}(a_i)
    \right\},
\]
where $\Delta_i$ is the applicable TTFT or completion planning budget.
If an RTT-scale burst is observed, at least two clusters contain an
SLO-feasible replica, and $i$ is non-LATENCY, RAVEL sets
\begin{equation}
    h_i
    =
    \min\!\left\{
        \rho_i^{\min},
        \sigma_i^{\mathrm{arr}}
    \right\},
    \label{eq:ravel-mobility-hold}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_i^{\min}$ is the minimum positive RTT to a remote serving cluster;
otherwise $h_i=0$.
LATENCY requests always use $h_i=0$.
The RTT bound keeps deliberate observation on a network timescale, while the
slack bound prevents RAVEL from intentionally waiting beyond the predicted
budget available to the request.
This is a bounded control choice rather than an optimality claim.

A held request is already tentatively placed and prospectively accounted.
Hold expiration only makes the request dispatch-eligible; it does not make
the placement final.
If handoff does not occur, the request remains router-owned and may continue
to participate in replanning.

At each pass, RAVEL orders eligible requests by
$\kappa_i=(a_i+\Delta_i,a_i,id_i)$ and sends a bounded prefix of at most $K$
requests to normal multi-request placement.
Completion soft admission may expand this cohort up to aggregate sequence
capacity.
Non-candidates retain their current owners as fixed planning context.
Candidate extraction may inspect router queues, so $K$ bounds the expensive
placement computation rather than every scheduling operation.
Within the cohort, earlier assignments update pass-local state before later
candidates are evaluated.


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{RAVEL} Router Control Loop}
\label{alg:ravel-control}
\small

\Fn{\textnormal{\textsc{OnArrival}}($i$)}{
    $Q_i \gets \textsc{QuoteReplicas}(i)$\;
    $r_i \gets \textsc{InitialPlacement}(i,Q_i)$\;
    \textsc{AssignAndAccount}($i,r_i$)\;
    \tcp{Demand becomes visible immediately}
    $h_i \gets \textsc{MobilityHold}(i,Q_i)$\;
    \If{$h_i = 0$}{
        \textsc{RequestReplan}()\;
    }
    \Else{
        \textsc{ArmReplan}($h_i$)\;
        \tcp{Placement remains revisable}
    }
}

\BlankLine

\Fn{\textnormal{\textsc{Replan}}()}{
    $C \gets \textsc{MovableCohort}()$\;
    \tcp{Only Router-owned work is movable}
    $X \gets \textsc{PlaceBySLOMode}(C)$\;
    \For{$i \in C$}{
        \textsc{TransferOrRefresh}($i,X_i$)\;
    }
}

\BlankLine

\Fn{\textnormal{\textsc{Dispatch}}($i$)}{
    \If{\textsc{Held}($i$) \textbf{or} \textsc{AdmissionBlocks}($i$)}{
        \Return\;
    }

    $r \gets \textsc{CurrentTentativeOwner}(i)$\;
    \textsc{RemoveFromRouterQueue}($i$)\;
    $s \gets \textsc{AttemptHandoff}(i,r)$\;

    \If{$s = \textsc{Rejected}$}{
        \textsc{RestoreRouterOwnership}($i,r$)\;
    }
    \ElseIf{$s = \textsc{Committed}$}{
        \textsc{FinalizePlacement}($i,r$)\;
        \tcp{Successful handoff ends mobility}
    }
    \Else{
        \textsc{SurfaceFailureAfterCleanup}($i$)\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{SLO-Specific Placement and Admission}
\label{sec:design:policy}

RAVEL selects its policy path using request-visible SLO and workflow semantics,
not workload names.
A multi-stage COLLECTIVE request is treated as workflow-completion traffic.
Mode selection is monotonic within a scheduler instance: a run may begin in
the completion-oriented path, but observing any request outside this predicate
switches subsequent placement to the mixed path for the remainder of that
instance.
This keeps completion-specific control scoped to runs that remain
semantically pure.

Completion \emph{soft admission} is narrower still.
It activates only for a pure workflow-completion stream after enough arrivals
have been observed for their measured rate to imply that aggregate sequence
capacity would fill within a pressure horizon equal to half of the tightest
observed completion budget, with a one-second minimum.
The exact sample and activation thresholds are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:completion-policy}.

\paragraph{Mixed TTFT/TBT traffic.}
The mixed path follows one principle:
\emph{preserve request-level SLO safety before balancing capacity}.
Its deterministic lexicographic placement has three conceptual tiers.
First, \emph{SLO safety} compares request-level feasibility, prospective
engine-admission overflow, and normalized objective and TTFT violation
pressure.
Second, \emph{capacity pressure} uses prospective TBT-admission and profiled
service signals to balance otherwise comparable targets.
Finally, \emph{efficiency and stability} consider normalized predicted
objective, convex marginal service, and placement movement.
Candidates are processed greedily in planning-deadline order, with each
selection updating the state seen by later candidates.
RAVEL therefore uses a bounded SLO-first online pass rather than an exact
global assignment solver or a fitted weighted score.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:mixed-policy} gives the exact deterministic key.

Tentative placement remains separate from engine admission.
RAVEL keeps the engine-visible Prefill frontier shallow so that excess work
remains router-owned and hence reassignable.
When LATENCY Decode is active, the serving adapter uses the tightest active
TBT requirement and a conservative calibrated Prefill envelope to bound the
next Chunked-Prefill quantum.
LATENCY requests receive deadline/TBT-aware local priority, whereas
non-LATENCY requests retain stable FCFS behavior.
These engine-local controls are not a second geographic placement policy;
they preserve the execution-time SLO assumptions on which RAVEL's geo-level
placement decisions rely.

\paragraph{Completion-oriented traffic.}
Under sustained completion demand, spending scarce service on requests already
unlikely to finish on time can reduce the number that ultimately satisfy their
deadlines.
RAVEL therefore follows a different overload principle:
\emph{protect service first for work that can still benefit from it}.

For the same candidate cohort and current planning context, RAVEL first runs
its ordinary SLO-aware greedy placement as a dry pass, without persisting the
completion virtual-service charges produced by that pass.
Let $X^0$ denote this baseline assignment.
RAVEL records its predicted-feasible cardinality
\begin{equation}
    B
    =
    \left|
        \left\{
            i\in C :
            \Phi_{i,X_i^0}=1
        \right\}
    \right|.
    \label{eq:ravel-protection-budget}
\end{equation}
$B$ is therefore a conservative reference produced by RAVEL's implemented
online baseline, not an approximation guarantee relative to the unknown
global maximum-cardinality assignment.
The completion planner attempts to preserve this cardinality while changing
which requests and replicas receive protection.

RAVEL scans candidates in planning-deadline order and admits only
predicted-feasible assignments to the protected set.
Among feasible choices it favors lower projected active-service load.
If a newcomer cannot otherwise be protected, RAVEL may replace the
largest-service protected request at a candidate replica, but only when the
newcomer remains feasible and the replacement frees positive profiled service.
The displaced request becomes \emph{deferred}, not rejected.
RAVEL retries deferred requests after the first scan; any request that still
cannot enter the protected set receives a best-effort tentative owner whose
quote is explicitly marked predicted infeasible.
Every candidate therefore retains a placement even when it is not protected.

During completion soft admission, the same held-out semantic expected-output
profile described above may be used for on-time-set planning; neither future
arrivals nor the evaluated request's realized output length are inputs to the
policy.
Protected requests cross the handoff frontier before deferred work.
Policy-level deferral is nevertheless bounded by
\begin{equation}
    t_i^{\mathrm{release}}
    =
    a_i+\Delta_i .
    \label{eq:ravel-soft-release}
\end{equation}
For completion traffic this equals the request's original completion-SLO
deadline.
It is \emph{not} a latest dispatch time at which RAVEL still predicts on-time
completion.
Rather, if a request remains deferred until this point, RAVEL stops
withholding it on behalf of the protected set and releases it to best-effort
admission; the policy no longer claims that the request can meet its original
completion SLO.
Protected work draining earlier may release it sooner.
Release makes the request dispatch-eligible but does not guarantee immediate
handoff, and RAVEL never drops a request merely because it is deferred.

After cohort construction, mixed placement performs
$O(|C||\mathcal R|)$ request--replica evaluations.
The completion path additionally searches protected work for replacements and
retries deferred requests, giving at most quadratic work in the bounded
cohort in the current implementation.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:overhead} measures the resulting online cost.


\subsection{Commitment and Engine Handoff}
\label{sec:design:commitment}

Tentative placement, mobility-hold expiration, dispatch eligibility, and
temporary router-queue removal are not commitment events.
For a dispatch-eligible request, RAVEL removes it from the router queue and
asks the tentative target to register the request in replica-side state.
If registration is rejected, the request is returned to its router queue and
remains eligible for ordinary scheduling and replanning.
After successful registration, RAVEL creates and tracks the final
engine-facing posting task.
Successful registration together with successful posting-task creation is
RAVEL's normal \emph{successful handoff}; only then does the placement become
final.

The implementation distinguishes partial and post-handoff failures.
If posting-task creation raises after replica registration, RAVEL aborts the
replica-side registration and cleans the associated task state rather than
treating the partial handoff as a successful dispatch.
Once the posting task has been successfully created and tracked, subsequent
task or serving errors belong to that committed attempt: RAVEL performs
terminal cleanup and updates planning state, but does not reinterpret the
failure as another placement opportunity.

Committed work remains visible to capacity planning.
Pending and running engine state continues to affect later quotes, and the
request's active-service charge persists until terminal completion.
Releasing that charge requests another scheduling pass so that newly available
capacity can affect requests that remain router-owned.

RAVEL therefore separates three lifecycle events:
\emph{arrival makes demand visible; router ownership keeps placement
revisable; successful engine handoff makes placement final.}
