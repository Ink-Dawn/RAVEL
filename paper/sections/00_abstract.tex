\subsection*{Abstract}

Regional capacity constraints and rising LLM inference demand are driving
providers to pool GPU resources across geographically distributed clusters,
where request routing must navigate heterogeneous WAN paths and serving
conditions.
Together with heterogeneous request-level SLOs, these differences make
placement feasibility request-specific: a region that is optional for one
request may be the only SLO-feasible choice for another.
Because SLO-feasible regions differ across requests, an arrival-time placement
does more than balance load: it may consume capacity that is substitutable for
the current request but indispensable to a later one.
Once finalized, such an allocation cannot be corrected when later arrivals
reveal its opportunity cost, even if the earlier request has not yet entered
the serving engine.
A natural response is to delay commitment.
We find that fixed-delay late binding initially improves SLO attainment but
eventually reverses the gain as the delay grows.
Our diagnosis shows that the dominant recoverable loss is not the direct cost
of waiting, but withholding already-arrived demand from subsequent capacity
planning.
This leads to RAVEL's key insight: each request should influence capacity
planning from the moment it arrives, while its placement remains revisable
until engine handoff.
RAVEL realizes this principle through tentative placement, immediate
prospective demand accounting, and bounded pre-handoff replanning.
On a vLLM deployment with five serving replicas across three clusters connected
by physical WAN paths, RAVEL improves request-level SLO attainment over the
best baseline at $16\times$ load by 8.8, 11.2, and 13.4 percentage points on
LMSYS, Burst, and DeepResearch, respectively, while reducing P95 TTFT by
49.1--80.5\% and P95 end-to-end latency by 15.5--56.8\%.
