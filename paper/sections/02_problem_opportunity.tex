\section{Problem and Design Opportunity}
\label{sec:problem}

Geo-distributed request placement is an online allocation problem in which
demand visibility and placement finality need not occur at the same time.
For request $i$, let $t_i^{\mathrm{vis}}$ denote the time at which the request
first contributes to the capacity state used for subsequent placement
decisions, and let $t_i^{\mathrm{final}}$ denote the time after which the
routing layer can no longer change its destination.
A placement associates the request with a candidate serving replica, whereas
visibility determines when its prospective demand influences later decisions.
These are logically distinct lifecycle events.
Because future arrivals are unknown, the router must choose placements and
their finality using only causally available demand and serving state, with the
goal of maximizing the fraction of requests that satisfy their applicable
request-level SLOs.


\subsection{Request-Dependent Regional Feasibility}
\label{sec:problem:feasibility}

SLO feasibility is specific to a request, replica, and decision time.
Prior SLO-aware serving systems show that whether a request can meet its
objective depends on request-specific requirements and evolving serving
conditions~\cite{distserve,jitserve,quartz,andes}.
At the geographic placement layer, these conditions additionally include the
WAN path to each candidate region.

Let $c$ denote a serving region, $\mathcal{R}_c$ the replicas in that region,
and $r\in\mathcal{R}_c$ a candidate replica.
For request $i$, let $\Phi_{i,r}(t)$ indicate whether replica $r$ is predicted,
using only information available at time $t$, to satisfy every SLO constraint
applicable to $i$.
The prediction depends on the request's SLO semantics and remaining slack,
estimated service demand, the WAN path to region $c$, and the observed serving
state at replica $r$.
Because these inputs evolve over time, $\Phi_{i,r}(t)$ is an online feasibility
estimate rather than a static property of a replica or region.
Section~\ref{sec:design:feasibility} describes the estimator used by RAVEL.

The feasible-region set captures the geographic alternatives available to a
request at a given decision time:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_i(t)
    =
    \left\{
        c
        \;\middle|\;
        \exists r\in\mathcal{R}_c:
        \Phi_{i,r}(t)=1
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:problem:feasible-set}
\end{equation}
The time index captures two sources of change.
First, new arrivals and accounting updates alter the serving state visible to
the router, so contention may expand or shrink $\mathcal{F}_i(t)$.
Second, elapsed time consumes TTFT or completion slack, so
$\mathcal{F}_i(t)$ may shrink even if serving load and network conditions remain
unchanged.

The size and composition of $\mathcal{F}_i(t)$ determine how substitutable
regional capacity is for request $i$.
A request with several feasible regions has alternatives, whereas one with a
small feasible set depends on comparatively scarce regional capacity.
For example, if
$\mathcal{F}_1=\{A,B\}$ and $\mathcal{F}_2=\{A\}$,
assigning request 1 to $A$ consumes request 2's only feasible option even though
request 1 could execute in $B$.
The opportunity cost of a region therefore depends on which requests can
substitute away from it.
An SLO failure can consequently be \emph{allocation-induced} rather than
necessarily \emph{capacity-induced}: an earlier request may occupy capacity
that it can replace elsewhere but a later request cannot.


\subsection{Coupling Demand Visibility with Commitment}
\label{sec:problem:tradeoff}

Later arrivals can change the opportunity cost of a placement that was
reasonable when it was made.
The router cannot know such arrivals in advance, and we do not assume an arrival
oracle or an offline-optimal allocation.
This setting shares concerns with online and shared-state cluster scheduling,
where decisions are made from incomplete and evolving information
~\cite{sparrow,flow_imprecise,omega,firmament}.
Any practical policy must therefore exploit only requests and serving state
that have become causally observable.

Demand visibility and placement finality are distinct lifecycle decisions.
Table~\ref{tab:problem:operating-points} illustrates three operating points.
A one-shot policy makes an arriving request visible immediately but also
finalizes its placement immediately, leaving no opportunity for later arrivals
to revise it.
Our diagnostic LateBind-$\tau$ policy instead holds the request centrally
unassigned and unaccounted for $\tau$, and then makes one final placement.
Thus,
\begin{equation}
    t_i^{\mathrm{vis}}
    =
    t_i^{\mathrm{final}}
\end{equation}
under both policies; they differ mainly in when this coupled event occurs.
The remaining operating point is to expose demand before placement becomes
irreversible.

Fixed-delay late binding shows that preserving optionality is useful, but
delaying commitment also introduces costs.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(a) shows that short deferral improves SLO attainment
by exposing additional arrivals, while the gain eventually reverses as
$\tau$ grows.
One intuitive cost is direct slack loss: holding request $i$ for $\tau$
consumes $\tau$ of its remaining TTFT or completion budget and can shrink
$\mathcal{F}_i(t)$ even when serving load is unchanged.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(b), however, shows that this direct waiting cost is
not the dominant recoverable penalty in our diagnostic.

The dominant recoverable loss comes from withholding already-arrived demand
from subsequent capacity planning.
At $\tau=80$~ms, eliminating the direct waiting penalty recovers only
0.6 percentage points of SLO attainment.
Keeping the same final-binding delay but immediately exposing held requests to
prospective accounting instead recovers 15.0 percentage points.
LateBind-$\tau$ therefore changes two lifecycle events simultaneously:
postponing finality preserves the opportunity to revise placement, but
postponing visibility causes later requests to plan against incomplete
prospective demand.
The desired operating point therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
    t_i^{\mathrm{vis}} < t_i^{\mathrm{final}},
    \label{eq:problem:visibility-before-finality}
\end{equation}
so that arrived work affects capacity planning before its placement becomes
irreversible.

Realizing this operating point safely requires more than simply delaying
commitment.
A practical design must
(i) estimate request-specific SLO feasibility so that reassignment considers
only viable alternatives;
(ii) make tentative demand visible exactly once and transfer its prospective
charge when placement changes;
(iii) exploit newly observed demand without unbounded waiting or global
replanning; and
(iv) define a failure-safe commitment boundary after which placement no longer
moves.
Section~\ref{sec:design} describes how RAVEL realizes these requirements and
chooses a concrete commitment boundary.
